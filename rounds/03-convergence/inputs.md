# Input Checklist for the Problem-Convergence Brief Case Study Skill (Round 3)

This document specifies what the user needs to provide for the Round 3 skill to produce a portfolio-grade brief. Round 3 has the steepest input requirements of the three rounds so far — it depends on completed Round 1 and Round 2 outputs, plus a structured team-scoring exercise, plus new market research, plus new field-deepened audit of an existing service. **Without these inputs, the skill will either ask you for them, leave clearly-flagged gaps, or produce a brief that fails the Scoring Benchmark on Convergence Rigor and Refinement Sharpness.**

There are two tiers of inputs:

- **Mandatory inputs** — without these, the skill cannot produce a credible Round 3. Do not skip these.
- **High-value optional inputs** — each significantly improves a specific section. The more of these you provide, the closer your output gets to portfolio-grade.

A fillable template is at the bottom. The recommended workflow: complete Rounds 1 and 2 first, then run a team-scoring session, then fill out the template, paste it alongside the Round 1 + Round 2 outputs, and invoke the skill.

---

## Why this matters

Round 3 is where the team **commits**. After two rounds of exploration, the team has to pick one problem area and defend that choice. The original Team Dua Round 3 brief works because:

- They actually ran an individual-scoring exercise (each team member scored 7 problems on 7 criteria — the radar chart shows their distinct polygons, not a team average)
- They rejected real problems with structured reasoning (PPD and Household Chores were both genuinely worth rejecting given their project's scope and timeline)
- They actually deepened their Anganwadi audit with quantified data (the 54.5% stat came from a real performance evaluation report)
- They actually pulled market research for childcare in India (the IMARC numbers are real)
- They actually refined the broad HMW into 3 sub-HMWs at distinct angles

Without analogous work in your project, Claude has two options: ask you to do the work first, or fabricate it. Neither is what you want.

**Treat this checklist as a 1-week investment between Round 2 and Round 3** — including 1 team scoring session (2–3 hours), 1–2 hours of market research, and a follow-up field visit if your Round 2 existing-services audit was thin on quantified data.

---

## Continuity requirements (these must already exist from prior rounds)

Before you fill out the Round 3 checklist, you should have:

**From Round 1:**
- A sharp HMW question (you will re-state this verbatim on Page 1)
- A defined target user
- Geography / regional context
- Team name and members
- Voice and visual style established

**From Round 2:**
- A problem mapping (mind-map of 5–8 problem categories with sub-issues) — the chosen problem on Round 3 must appear here
- A stakeholder map (you will use this when building the new ecosystem map)
- An existing-services audit (you will deepen the Anganwadi-style section in Round 3)
- Two contrasting personas

If any of these are missing or unstable, return to the prior skills before continuing.

## Mandatory inputs

### 1. Round 1 HMW verbatim + Round 2 problem mapping summary

**What:** Paste your Round 1 HMW verbatim, plus a list of the 5–8 problem categories from your Round 2 mind-map.

**Example:**
- HMW: "How might we help working women who are in early stages of motherhood attain a sustainable work-life balance?"
- Round 2 problem categories: Occupational, Financial, Mental Health, Child Personal, Physical Health, Transport

**Why it matters:** The HMW callout on Page 1 is built from this. The candidate problems for scoring are pulled from the Round 2 problem categories.

---

### 2. 6–8 candidate problems for team scoring

**What:** A shortlist of 6–8 specific problems from your Round 2 research that the team is considering as the focus for ideation.

**Example:** Breast Feeding, Daycare, Household Chores, Lack of Nutrition, Hygiene, Transport, Post-Partum Depression

**Why it matters:** These become the axes on the radar chart. Each must be specific enough to score on the 7 criteria but broad enough that a Round 4 solution could meaningfully address it.

**Common mistake:** Listing categories (e.g., "Physical Health") rather than specific problems (e.g., "Post-Pregnancy Complications"). Specifics beat categories.

---

### 3. Scoring criteria (default available)

**What:** The criteria the team will score each candidate problem on. Use the default 7 criteria unless your project has different ones.

**Default criteria:**
1. **Feasibility** — can the team realistically address this in available time + resources?
2. **Excitement** — does the team genuinely want to work on it?
3. **Scalability** — can a solution serve many users?
4. **Business Viability** — is there a sustainable revenue or funding model?
5. **Impact** — how meaningfully does solving this improve user well-being?
6. **Sustainability** — does the solution maintain value over time?
7. **Interconnectivity** — does solving this also alleviate other connected problems?

**Why it matters:** These criteria are bolded on Page 1's Problem Scoring intro sentence. They establish the rationality of the scoring process.

---

### 4. Team scoring data (per team member, per problem, per criterion)

**What:** Each team member's individual scores for each candidate problem on each criterion. For 4 team members × 7 problems × 7 criteria = 196 individual scores. Use a 1–5 or 1–10 scale.

**How to run the scoring session:**
- Hour 1: Everyone individually scores all 7 problems on all 7 criteria. No discussion yet.
- Hour 2: Compare scores. Note where the team agrees and where it diverges.
- Hour 3: Discuss divergences. Optionally let people update scores after hearing rationale. Save both pre- and post-discussion scores if you want to show evolution.

**Why it matters:** The radar chart shows individual team-member polygons overlaid. This quantifies consensus rigorously — you can see which problems had tight agreement (all polygons overlap) and which had divergence (one polygon extends much further than others).

**Common mistake:** Skipping the individual-scoring step and just having a discussion that produces team averages. This loses the consensus signal and weakens the radar chart's value.

---

### 5. The chosen problem + 2 rejected problems with reasoning

**What:** Based on the scoring, identify your final chosen problem plus 2 problems that scored well but were rejected. For each, write 2–4 sentences of reasoning.

**Example (rejected — scope mismatch):** *"Postpartum Depression is a multifaceted mental health issue with stigma and limited awareness. Working on this problem requires specialized knowledge and skills that cannot be acquired in a short period of time. Moreover Postpartum Depression is not exclusive to employed mothers."*

**Example (rejected — low interconnectivity):** *"The issue of household chores extends beyond employed mothers and the burden of household tasks varies significantly among mothers. Addressing this issue may yield a limited impact as it is not closely interlinked with other challenges faced by working mothers."*

**Example (chosen — positive justification):** *"We have identified the primary apprehension of mothers, as the welfare of their child, which directly impacts their professional obligations. Childcare is closely interlinked with a number of many other smaller problems yet this issue remains largely neglected."*

**Why it matters:** Page 1's Narrowing Down section is built from this. The rejection arguments need to be respectful, not dismissive — "this problem is real, but doesn't fit our project scope" is the tone.

**Rejection reasoning vocabulary** (use one or more for each rejected problem):
- Scope mismatch with the user: "not exclusive to [USER]," "extends beyond [USER]"
- Specialization required: "requires specialized knowledge that cannot be acquired in a short period"
- Low interconnectivity: "may yield a limited impact as it is not closely interlinked with other challenges"
- High stigma / sensitivity: "is a multifaceted [TYPE] with stigma and limited awareness"

**Acceptance reasoning vocabulary:**
- Tight user fit: "directly impacts their [PROFESSIONAL/PERSONAL] obligations," "is the primary apprehension"
- High interconnectivity: "is closely interlinked with a number of other problems"
- Under-addressed: "yet this issue remains largely neglected"

---

### 6. Pull quote for the chosen problem (Page 1 closer)

**What:** One new interview quote that emotionally anchors the chosen problem, with dual-identity attribution (e.g., "A Developer & A Mother").

**Example:** "It's not easy for a mother to go back to work, even though my son was almost 1 year old when I joined back work, I could never feel comfortable leaving him behind." — Payal Sinha, A Developer & A Mother.

**Why it matters:** This quote closes Page 1 by giving the chosen problem an emotional anchor before the brief moves into the analytical ecosystem map on Page 2.

**Critical:** Must be a verbatim quote from a real interview. No paraphrasing or invention.

---

### 7. Ecosystem map themes (5–7 themes for the chosen problem area)

**What:** The 5–7 thematic categories that organize the chosen problem space. Should be domain-spanning nouns, not actions.

**Example (for childcare):** Care Facilities, Physical Health, Mental Health, Development, Transport, Nutrition. Plus optionally a cross-cutting theme like Government (regulatory / policy).

**Example (for elderly daily companionship):** Social Connection, Mental Wellness, Cultural / Spiritual Practice, Daily Routines, Physical Activity, Technology Bridge. Plus optionally a Government theme.

**Why it matters:** These become the radial branches of the Page 2 ecosystem map. Each theme will have associated needs, touchpoints, and stakeholders.

---

### 8. Ecosystem map data per theme (needs, touchpoints, stakeholders)

**What:** For each theme, provide:
- **Needs** (5–12 per theme): the user's specific requirements within that theme, with a note on which are highest impact (will be placed closer to the center on the map)
- **Touchpoints** (1–5 per theme): specific interaction points or artifacts (e.g., "Daycare Websites," "Diet Charts," "Therapy")
- **Stakeholders** (2–8 per theme): people, organizations, or institutions involved, with sub-categorization if applicable (Public / Private / Government)

**Example (for the Care Facilities theme under childcare):**
- Needs (high → low impact): Empathetic Treatment, Affordability, Availability of Seats, Reliability, Trustworthy, Flexible, Accessible, Child-Proofing, Proper Surveillance, Cultural Connect, Formal Updates, Individual attention, Same Aged Peers
- Touchpoints: Daycare Websites, News/Articles, Word of Mouth
- Stakeholders: Daycares/Crèches (Anganwadis Public, Workplace Private, Private), Family, Caretakers

**Why it matters:** Without this granular data, the ecosystem map will be sparse — defeating the page's purpose of showing comprehensive coverage of the chosen problem area.

**How to gather:** Affinity-map your Round 2 interview notes + field observations against your 5–7 themes. Every requirement, touchpoint, or stakeholder mentioned across your research becomes an item in the appropriate category.

---

### 9. Inter-theme linkages (4+ strong/weak connections between themes)

**What:** A list of 4–8 linkages between themes, marked as strong or weak.

**Example:**
- Strong: Care Facilities ↔ Mental Health (good care reduces mother's anxiety)
- Strong: Nutrition ↔ Physical Health (nutritional gaps cause physical issues)
- Strong: Government ↔ Care Facilities (regulations directly shape facility quality)
- Weak: Transport ↔ Mental Health (transport stress affects mood but indirectly)

**Why it matters:** These linkages are the *argumentative* element of the ecosystem map. They justify why this problem was chosen as "closely interlinked" — a key reason cited on Page 1.

**Common mistake:** Not specifying linkages, resulting in themes that float in isolation on the map. Without linkages, the interconnectivity argument falls flat.

---

### 10. Pain points list (15+ specific pain points of the existing service)

**What:** A list of 15+ pain points specific to the existing service category for your chosen problem. Each pain point is 3–7 words.

**Example (for daycare):** Inability to build trust, Lack of reliable transport service, Infections spread easily, Open only for regular working hours, No standardized regulations by the government, Incorrect Staff-to-child ratio, Lack of Vacancy, Unaffordable, Do Not Provide Meals, Lack of Transparency, Not accessible for all, No Certifications or trainings available, No curriculum or play school integration, Homely environment missing, Do not ensure overall development, Lack of companionship, No outdoor activities or play areas

**Why it matters:** These will orbit a central service-category icon on Page 3. The visual argument is: "The pain belongs to the existing system, not the user."

**Critical:** Pain points must be specific to the existing service category — not generic life issues. Don't list "stress" or "burnout"; list "Open only for regular working hours" or "Incorrect Staff-to-child ratio."

**How to gather:** Pull pain points from your Round 2 interview notes specifically about the existing service experience, supplemented by any new field visits or interviews focused on the chosen problem area.

---

### 11. 2 new pull quotes specific to the existing service experience

**What:** 2 new interview quotes from mothers (or your target user) speaking about their direct experience with the existing service category. Each with dual-identity attribution.

**Example:**
- "A child with cough and cold cannot be stopped from entering daycare, but since it spreads easily, it results in a weak immune system of all the kids." — Shreya Singh, A Data Analyst & A Mother
- "Pretty much anyone who calls themselves nanny is allowed to work at a daycare and we don't have any information exposed to their past or work experience." — Divya Gupta, A Sr. Manager & A Mother

**Why it matters:** These quotes are the empirical evidence for the pain points listed above them. Without them, the pain points are claims; with them, the pain points are observations.

---

### 12. Regional scenario specific to the chosen problem (Page 3)

**What:** 2–3 short paragraphs describing the cultural and infrastructural reality of your chosen problem area in your geography.

**Required content:**
- Cultural perception of the chosen problem (often negative — e.g., "daycare is often viewed negatively")
- Cultural attribution that needs unlearning (e.g., "viewed as a way to avoid parenting duties")
- Infrastructure gap that compounds the cultural issue (e.g., rural areas relying on informal arrangements)

**Why it matters:** This section in Round 3 must zoom *narrower* than Rounds 1 and 2 — specifically to the chosen problem's cultural treatment. If your Round 3 scenario could be pasted from Round 2, you haven't zoomed in.

---

### 13. Setbacks of an existing service — with quantified failure data

**What:** Deepen your Round 2 existing-services audit by providing:
- Service name (same as in Round 2's audit, e.g., Anganwadi)
- 4 specific failure modes (each 2–4 words, e.g., "Low Space," "Shortage of Caretakers," "Underqualified Staff," "Improper Hygiene")
- A specific operational-failure paragraph (e.g., dietary requirements, closure times, supervision)
- One quantified failure statistic with source (e.g., "Only 54.5% enrolled children attended regularly across 21 AWCs. Source: Report on Evaluation of the Performance of Anganwadi")
- 1–2 closing observations (e.g., enrollment-only-due-to-regulations, overworked employees)

**Why it matters:** Round 3 must go deeper than Round 2 on this audit. Round 2's pros/cons get replaced by Round 3's specific failures with quantified evidence.

**How to gather:** Find a real performance evaluation report of the existing service. Government audits, NGO evaluations, academic studies, journalism investigations. Cite the source.

**Common mistake:** Repeating Round 2's cons list. If that's all you have, you haven't deepened — go find one quantified failure metric from a real report.

---

### 14. 5 impact statements (individual → society ripple)

**What:** 5 sentences each naming a different layer of impact from solving the chosen problem. The 5 should span an expanding ripple:

1. **Primary beneficiary** — what improves for the most directly affected person (e.g., the child)
2. **HMW user** — what improves for the user in the original HMW (e.g., the working mother)
3. **Family / household** — what improves for the user's household
4. **Adjacent affected parties** — who else benefits indirectly (e.g., older siblings, grandparents)
5. **Society / structure** — what changes at the systemic level (e.g., gender equality, economic growth)

**Example pattern for each:** A 2–3 sentence paragraph with the key impact phrase in bold and a clear causal mechanism.

**Why it matters:** Page 4's Impact section is built from this. Without explicit 5-layer ripple structure, impact statements collapse into platitudes.

---

### 15. Business Viability — 3 market arguments + 2 hard market data points

**What:**
- 3 market arguments, each 1–2 sentences with bolded key phrases:
  1. Quality gap in current solutions = innovation opportunity
  2. Demand evidenced by willingness to pay
  3. Demographic / structural tailwind making solution increasingly necessary

- 2 hard market data points with a real market research source:
  - Market Size (with current + projected year values)
  - Market Growth Rate / CAGR (with year range)
  - Source: a named market research firm (IMARC, Statista, Mordor Intelligence, Grand View Research, McKinsey, BCG, etc.)

**Example:**
- Market Size: 2022 (US$ 3.8 Billion) → 2028 (US$ 7.3 Billion)
- Market Growth Rate: 11.2% during 2023–2028
- Source: Report by IMARC Group

**Why it matters:** Page 4's Business Viability section is built from this. This is the new framing in Round 3 — commercial alongside social.

**If no commercial market exists** (public-goods problems, policy interventions, advocacy work): reframe as "Funding Viability" — naming who pays for or sustains the intervention (government budgets, philanthropic capital, cross-subsidy). Be honest about this — don't force a market frame if it isn't natural.

**Common mistake:** Fabricating market sizes. If you can't find real data, ask Claude to search for it, or reframe the section as Funding Viability.

---

### 16. 3 refined sub-HMW questions

**What:** 3 sub-HMW questions that split your Round 1 HMW into 3 different solution-space angles.

**Example (childcare):**
1. **Well-being / holistic angle:** "How might we prioritise the mental and physical well-being of both the mother and the child while also ensuring proper nutrition?"
2. **Developmental / social angle:** "How might we help provide children with consistent interaction, cultural connection, and peer relationships in a fast-paced society?"
3. **Operational / service-reliability angle:** "How might we help employed mothers find a well maintained, safe and reliable childcare service which ensures proper care while accommodating their busy schedules?"

**Why it matters:** Page 4's How Might We section is built from this. The 3 sub-HMWs become the prompts for Round 4 ideation.

**Test for whether they're distinct:** If answering one sub-HMW would automatically answer another, the two aren't sufficiently distinct. Rewrite.

**Test for whether they're sharp:** Each sub-HMW must be a valid stand-alone HMW the team could ideate against — not a stub that requires combining with the others to be meaningful.

---

### 17. Formal Need Statement (User + Need + Insight format)

**What:** A single-sentence need statement using the formula:

> **[USER]** need **[SPECIFIC NEED — qualities of the desired service]** so that **[INSIGHT — deeper outcome, ideally two-part]**.

**Example:**
> "**Employed mothers** need **a reliable service that priorities mother-child well-being and nutrition while providing children with consistent interaction & connection** so that **they can feel safe about their child and thrive in their career**."

**Construction rules:**
- **User:** same as Round 1's HMW user (scoped life-phase user)
- **Need:** names *qualities* of the desired service (reliable, prioritises X, provides Y), NOT its *form* (an app, a community center)
- **Insight (the "so that" clause):** the deeper outcome — ideally two-part: anxiety relieved + aspiration enabled

**Why it matters:** This is the formal artifact that will guide all of Round 4's ideation. The "so that" insight is the success criterion for the eventual solution.

**Common mistake:** Naming a solution form ("Mothers need an app that…"). Forms are for Round 4.

---

### 18. Team credit line (same as Rounds 1 and 2)

**What:** "Team [NAME]: [Member 1], [Member 2], [Member 3], [Member 4]"

**Why it matters:** Appears at bottom of Page 4 in italic styling, consistent with Rounds 1 and 2.

## High-value optional inputs

### 19. Pre-discussion and post-discussion scoring data (for the radar chart)

**What:** If you ran the scoring session in two phases (individual scoring + discussion + revised scoring), provide both sets of scores. The radar chart could show either, or both as overlays.

**Why it matters:** Showing evolution from pre- to post-discussion is a richer consensus signal than either alone.

---

### 20. Field visit notes for the setbacks section

**What:** If your team did a recent field visit to the existing service (e.g., visited an Anganwadi to observe operations), include observations.

**Why it matters:** The most credible setbacks are first-hand observations, not just secondary report findings.

---

### 21. Additional stakeholder quotes for the ecosystem map

**What:** Quotes from non-user stakeholders specific to the chosen problem (e.g., a daycare operator, a pediatrician focused on daycare-related illness).

**Why it matters:** Strengthens the ecosystem map's credibility — each stakeholder branch has a voice attached.

---

### 22. Specific source links for market data

**What:** URLs or report identifiers for the market research you're citing.

**Why it matters:** Lets Claude verify the data and cite it precisely.

---

## How to gather these inputs

**For the candidate problems:** Pull from your Round 2 problem mapping. Look for the 6–8 sub-issues that surfaced most repeatedly across interviews + field observations + secondary research.

**For the scoring session:** Block 3 hours with the full team. Hour 1: individual scoring (no discussion). Hour 2: review divergences. Hour 3: discuss and optionally revise. Save all individual scoring data.

**For rejection reasoning:** Look at problems that scored well but didn't make the final cut. For each, identify which of the rejection-vocabulary patterns applies (scope mismatch / specialization / interconnectivity / stigma) and write the reasoning from there.

**For ecosystem map data:** Affinity-map all your Round 2 research notes against your 5–7 themes. Every requirement, touchpoint, or stakeholder mentioned becomes an item.

**For inter-theme linkages:** Ask "if we improved theme A, would it also improve theme B?" If yes — and the team can name the mechanism — that's a strong link. If yes but indirectly — that's a weak link.

**For pain points:** Sit with your Round 2 interview notes for an hour and extract every complaint specific to the existing service category. Aim for 20+ items, then keep the 15 most distinct.

**For market data:** Search "[Chosen problem area] India market size 2024" or similar. IMARC, Statista, Mordor Intelligence, Grand View Research are good starting points. Most reports have free executive summaries that include the key numbers.

**For the need statement:** Draft it last, after everything else is in place. Re-read your Round 1 HMW + your sub-HMWs + your impact statements. The need statement is the synthesis of all three.

## Common input mistakes

1. **Skipping the individual-scoring step.** You bring team-aggregated scores to Claude. The radar chart can't show team-member-specific polygons, weakening the rigor argument. Fix: re-run the scoring session with individual scoring first.

2. **Chosen problem isn't in Round 2's mind-map.** You picked a focus that wasn't in your prior research. Fix: either go back to Round 2 and expand the mind-map, or pick a chosen problem already present.

3. **Rejection reasoning is dismissive.** "PPD isn't really important." Fix: rewrite as scope-mismatch or resource-realism — "is not exclusive to [USER]" or "requires specialized knowledge cannot be acquired in a short period."

4. **Pain points are generic life-issues, not service-failures.** Fix: pain points must be specific to the existing service category. "Stress" doesn't qualify; "Open only for regular working hours" does.

5. **Market data without source.** Fix: every market data point needs a real, named market research firm and report. If you can't find one, reframe as Funding Viability.

6. **Sub-HMWs are synonyms.** Fix: each should occupy a different solution-space angle (holistic / developmental / operational).

7. **Need Statement names a solution form.** "Mothers need a daycare-booking app that…" Fix: name *qualities* of the service, not the form. "Mothers need a reliable service that prioritises..."

8. **Setbacks section repeats Round 2's pros/cons.** Fix: Round 3 should have at least one new quantified failure stat from a real evaluation report.

9. **Ecosystem map data is sparse.** Themes have 2-3 items in each category. Fix: aim for 5+ needs and 2+ touchpoints and 2+ stakeholders per theme. Density is the argument.

10. **No inter-theme linkages.** Themes float in isolation. Fix: identify 4+ linkages with mechanisms — these justify the "closely interlinked" claim from Page 1.

---

## What happens at each input quality level

- **Mandatory inputs only:** Competent draft that may struggle on Theme-Ecosystem Depth (if linkages or theme data is thin) and on Refinement Sharpness (if market data is missing).

- **Mandatory + half the optional inputs:** Strong draft that mostly hits Scoring Benchmark targets. Some sections feel more "yours" than others.

- **All inputs filled in:** Portfolio-grade draft where every page reflects your team's specific Round 3 research. The Scoring Benchmark targets should all be met on the first pass.

The 1-week investment of running the scoring session + doing market research + revisiting the existing service for quantified failure data is the highest-leverage move you can make for Round 3.
