## Page 1

### Introduction

We initiated our research by exploring the concept of **wellbeing** and the different perspectives and meanings it holds for different individuals and groups. We conducted desk research across published reports and academic studies, semi-structured interviews with working mothers, children of working mothers, and domain experts, and exploratory brainstorming sessions to gain a deeper understanding of the topic. Through this process, we were able to discover that **work-life balance for women in early motherhood**, particularly with respect to the tension between career continuity and caregiving expectations, is vastly under-represented and overlooked in current workplace and policy discussions.

Our objective is to design a service that addresses the specific needs of **working women in the early stages of motherhood** in terms of their daily wellbeing, and improves their overall work-life balance.

### The Global Scenario

The global scenario for **women's workplace participation** has seen major progress in recent years, with female labor force participation rising steadily and more countries adopting maternity protections and flexible work arrangements. However, working mothers still face a persistent gap between policy on paper and support in practice — they are disproportionately expected to absorb caregiving duties while maintaining professional output at the same standard as their peers without children.

The COVID-19 pandemic sharpened this disparity. Women with young children experienced higher rates of job exit and burnout than any other demographic group, and recovery has been uneven — mothers of children under six participate in the labor force at roughly 53%, compared with over 90% for fathers in the same age range.

**The challenges working mothers face:**

| Personal & Occupational | Family & Caregiving |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| Career stagnation | Childcare logistics |
| Workplace bias after maternity | Guilt over child's development |
| Loss of professional identity | Household management load |
| Commute and time rigidity | Lack of dependable support systems |

> **43%**
> of women globally are not working because they are occupied with childcare and household duties.
> *Source: World Bank, Women and Work Data (2024)*

> **77%**
> of working mothers have experienced burnout at their current job, significantly higher than the workforce average.
> *Source: Hubstaff, Work-Life Balance Statistics (2024)*

### The Indian Perspective

While many developed economies have established systems for **paid parental leave**, subsidized childcare, and return-to-work support, India is still struggling to provide even basic structural support for working mothers. There exist numerous barriers — cultural, economic, and institutional — which lead to women exiting the workforce at rates far higher than their global counterparts.

> **7 in 10**
> Indians believe that a woman's salary is secondary to her husband's and that her primary duty lies in managing the household.
> *Source: FSG GLOW Report, "Creating a Gender-Equitable Workforce in India"*

> **58%**
> of women employees in India quit their jobs due to increased family responsibilities in the last two years.
> *Source: Business Insider India (2022)*

### Impact on Working Mothers in Early Motherhood

While there are many reasons for women to leave the workforce, **childcare and family responsibility** has often been recognised as a primary cause. Studies show that the majority of women citing this cause are those with young children — specifically, women navigating the **early stages of motherhood**, when the demands of an infant or toddler collide with an employer's expectation of full-time availability.

> **30%**
> of mothers with children under 6 participate in the workforce, compared to significantly higher rates among women without young children — a gap that widens in urban nuclear families where extended family support is absent.
> *Source: IWWAGE India Factsheet (2024)*

> **74%**
> of India's educated women take career breaks, with motherhood cited as the dominant trigger for the first break.
> *Source: HRKatha Research (2025)*

### Interview Insights

We interviewed **working mothers currently employed, mothers who left their jobs, children of working mothers (ages 8 and 14), and a practicing psychologist who is also a mother** to triangulate perspectives on this experience.

**[Icon: clock with overlapping arrows]**
Working mothers face significant **time management** challenges. Their days are structured around two competing schedules — the employer's and the child's — with no buffer between. Several mothers described arriving at work already exhausted from the morning routine and leaving early with guilt about unfinished tasks on both sides.

**[Icon: two figures, one larger passing a bundle to the smaller]**
Despite supportive intentions, most families still treat childcare as **primarily the woman's responsibility**. Husbands described themselves as "helping out" rather than sharing ownership, and extended family — when available — offered advice more often than practical support. The structural assumption that the mother is the default caregiver persists even in progressive households.

**[Icon: heart with a crack or weight]**
Across interviews, the most consistent theme was a deep sense of **stress and guilt**. Mothers described feeling inadequate in both roles simultaneously — not present enough for their child, not committed enough for their employer. This emotional weight is invisible to colleagues and often minimized by family members who see only the external logistics.

> "People need to understand that she is a human before she's a mother. A woman's needs are always neglected, she's never a human being and always has a role to play."
> *— Shikha Handa, A Psychologist & A Mother*

Children, too, carry an awareness of their mother's struggle. The older child we interviewed described **noticing when her mother was stressed** and trying to "be less trouble" — a form of emotional labor that children should not need to perform. The younger child expressed it more simply: she wished her mother could pick her up from school more often.

Despite these difficulties, many working mothers still find a deep sense of **purpose and identity** in their professional lives. Work is not merely an economic necessity — it is a source of self-worth, social connection, and personal growth. Several mothers said that quitting would solve the logistics but worsen the emotional picture. The challenge is not that they want to stop working; it is that the systems around them make continuing unsustainably hard.

### Emotional Arc

The emotional arc depicts the various stages of a **working mother's** life from the moment she learns about her pregnancy till the point where career sustainability becomes untenable. It encompasses the various emotions and thought processes she goes through as a **professional navigating new motherhood**.

**Here we focus on new mothers who leave or seriously consider leaving work to take care of their child.** This is one trajectory, not the universal one — mothers with strong support systems, flexible employers, or different life circumstances may follow a different arc.

**5 Stages:**

**Stage 1: Pregnancy**
- Gets pregnant (cautious optimism)
- Difficulties during pregnancy while working (stress rises)
- Excitement about the baby (brief emotional high)

**Stage 2: Maternity Leave**
- Works until the third trimester (determination mixed with physical strain)
- Takes maternity leave (relief, bonding)
- Worries about job security during leave (anxiety creeps in)

**Stage 3: Return to the Job**
- Has to return after 6 months (emotional wrench)
- No proper policies on maternal care at workplace (frustration)
- Gives quitting her job a thought (low point)
- Finds a temporary rhythm (slight recovery)

**Stage 4: Finding Child Care**
- Unavailability of credible childcare options (anxiety spikes)
- Settles for an unsatisfactory arrangement (resignation)
- Child falls ill or shows distress at daycare (guilt surge)

**Stage 5: Career Challenges**
- Passed over for promotion or project (resentment, self-doubt)
- Triggered by child struggling or missing milestones (emotional breaking point)
- Eventually leaves the job (grief mixed with relief)

**Trajectory shape:** Descending zigzag — emotional highs are followed by deeper lows. The overall trend is downward, arguing that the system fails the mother gradually through accumulated friction, not a single catastrophic event.

**Emotion curve touchpoints (12 points):**
1. Gets pregnant — moderate positive
2. Difficulties during pregnancy — slight negative
3. Excitement about baby — positive spike
4. Works until third trimester — moderate negative
5. Maternity leave bonding — positive
6. Worries about job security — negative
7. Returns to work — sharp negative
8. Finds temporary rhythm — slight positive recovery
9. Childcare search fails — negative, deeper than previous lows
10. Child shows distress — sharp negative (lowest point so far)
11. Passed over at work — deep negative
12. Eventually leaves job — deepest point, slight uptick of relief

**Visual elements to design:**
- Full-page layout, landscape orientation recommended
- 5 columns (one per stage), with stage labels as column headers in display weight
- A continuous emotion curve (Y-axis: happy to distressed, X-axis: time) threading through all 5 columns
- 12 plotted touchpoints on the curve, each with:
  - A short label (3-6 words) connected by a thin line
  - An emoji indicator at the point (use a happy/neutral/sad face to repeat the emotional state visually)
- 3 anchor icons scattered across the arc:
  - A baby icon at Stage 1 (Pregnancy)
  - A briefcase icon at Stage 3 (Return to the Job)
  - A parent-child icon at Stage 4 (Finding Child Care)
- Soft pastel background, coral accent on the curve line, cream background on the columns
- The scope disclaimer sentence rendered in a small highlighted box at the top of the page

### Types of Wellbeing Affected

The relationship between the **six dimensions of wellbeing** — Physical, Emotional, Environmental, Social, Financial, and Occupational — is deeply interconnected for working mothers. A disruption in one dimension cascades into others: occupational stress triggers emotional exhaustion, which affects physical health, which strains social relationships.

However, our focus is primarily on promoting **Emotional** and **Occupational** wellbeing. By addressing these two areas, we anticipate a positive ripple effect on **Social**, **Physical**, and **Financial** wellbeing over time.

**Visual elements to design:**
- A 6-point radar/spider chart with the six wellbeing dimensions as vertices
- Emotional and Occupational vertices highlighted (filled in coral), other vertices in light grey outline
- A shaded polygon connecting all 6 points to show the interconnection, with Emotional and Occupational pulling the shape outward (highest scores / highest focus)
- Small explanatory labels on Emotional and Occupational: "Primary focus"
- Small labels on Social and Physical: "Expected ripple"

### Opportunities Identified

Based on our research, we have determined that **trust and flexibility** are crucial elements for working mothers that are currently lacking in the systems around them. When mothers cannot trust the quality of their child's care and cannot flex their schedules around caregiving demands, the system breaks down — not all at once, but through the accumulated friction our emotional arc documented.

Three directional opportunity areas emerge:

1. **Modifying existing work policies** — toward maternal-stage-aware flexibility rather than one-size-fits-all leave
2. **Providing support within community** — activating neighborhood, family, and peer networks as a caregiving layer
3. **Improving childcare facilities** — raising quality, accessibility, and trustworthiness of early childhood care infrastructure

**Visual elements to design:**
- A branching diagram with a central node ("Opportunities") and three branches, each ending in a rounded rectangle with the opportunity label
- Each branch has a small icon: a policy/document icon, a community/people icon, and a building/facility icon
- The three branches should visually span different directions (not a vertical list) to emphasize they target different systemic layers
- Color: coral accent on the central node, cream backgrounds on the three opportunity cards

### Problem Statement & Expected Impact

> **"How might we help working women who are in early stages of motherhood attain a sustainable work-life balance?"**

As **nuclear families** continue to increase in prevalence across urban India, the informal support structures that once absorbed childcare — grandparents, extended family, community networks — are disappearing. Working mothers are left negotiating an impossible equation between employer expectations calibrated to an unencumbered worker and caregiving demands calibrated to a full-time parent. This structural shift calls for intentional, designed support systems that treat work-life balance not as a personal problem to be managed, but as a systemic gap to be addressed.

**Expected Impact:**

1. **Individual:** Reduced stress and improved mental and physical health for working mothers
2. **Child & Family:** Positive impact on the development and wellbeing of the child through more present, less exhausted parenting
3. **Workplace:** Better familial relationships and increased efficiency at work, reducing absenteeism and attrition
4. **Societal:** Making it easier for more women to join or rejoin the workforce, contributing to India's economic growth and gender equity goals
