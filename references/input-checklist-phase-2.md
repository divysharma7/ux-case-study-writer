# Input Checklist for the Empathy-Deepening Brief Case Study Skill (Round 2)

This document specifies what the user needs to provide for the Round 2 skill to produce a portfolio-grade brief. Round 2 is more research-intensive than Round 1 — the brief depends on real fieldwork, real bodystorming, and real second-wave interviews. **Without these inputs, the skill will either ask you for them, leave clearly-flagged gaps, or produce a brief that fails the Scoring Benchmark on Ecosystem Breadth and Empathic Granularity.**

There are two tiers of inputs:

- **Mandatory inputs** — without these, the skill cannot produce a credible Round 2. Do not skip these.
- **High-value optional inputs** — each significantly improves a specific section. The more of these you provide, the closer your output gets to portfolio-grade.

A fillable template is at the bottom. The recommended workflow: fill out the template, paste it into your conversation with Claude alongside your Round 1 brief, and invoke the skill.

---

## Why this matters

Round 1 was a desk-research + interview synthesis. Round 2 requires **fieldwork, bodystorming, and a wider second-wave of interviews**. The original Team Dua Round 2 brief works because:

- They actually visited an Anganwadi (the existing-services audit is sourced to "Field Visits and Interviews")
- They actually interviewed 5+ stakeholder roles (Gynecologist, Pediatrician, Spouse, Child, Caretaker — each surfaces a distinct angle)
- They actually bodystormed (4 team members each simulated a day in the life of an employed mother — the wavy lines on Page 4 are real)
- They actually changed their minds (the 3 invalidated assumptions are genuine — beliefs they entered with that the research disproved)

Without analogous fieldwork in your project, Claude has two options: ask you to do the fieldwork first (which slows you down), or fabricate it (which fails the Scoring Benchmark and is a research integrity violation). Neither is what you want.

**Treat this checklist as a 1–2 week research investment between Round 1 and Round 2.** If you skip the research, the brief will read as a desk exercise rather than a research deliverable.

---

## Continuity requirements (these must already exist from Round 1)

Before you fill out the Round 2 checklist, you should have the following from Round 1:

- A sharp HMW question
- A defined target user (life-phase + role)
- Geography / regional context
- Team name and member names
- Voice and visual style established

If any of these are missing or unstable, return to the `discovery-brief-case-study` skill before continuing.

## Mandatory inputs

### 1. Round 1 HMW question and context

**What:** Paste your Round 1 HMW question verbatim, plus the target user description and geography.

**Why it matters:** Round 2 is an expansion of Round 1, not a new project. Every section of Round 2 should be traceable back to the HMW. If Claude doesn't know the HMW, it will infer one — and you may end up with a Round 2 that doesn't match where you actually are.

**Common mistake:** Pivoting the target user mid-project. If you've changed your mind about the user, that's a Round 1 redo, not a Round 2.

---

### 2. Filtration funnel stages (Page 1)

**What:** 6–8 life or career stages showing how your user gets filtered out of opportunities. Listed in order from most-inclusive to most-restrictive.

**Example (for working women):** School Students → University → University Students → Employment → Employed → Continue work after Marriage → Continue work after Children → Pregnancy

**Example (for elderly loneliness):** Healthy aging with spouse → Spouse's illness → Loss of spouse → Adult children move out → Mobility decline → Isolation

**Why it matters:** Page 1's filtration funnel visualization is built from this. Without these stages, Claude will either generalize ("women face barriers throughout life") or fabricate stages.

**How to derive yours:** List the major life transitions where your target user typically loses access to something (opportunity, support, agency, etc.). The stages should be specific enough that someone in that life could point to themselves at one of them.

---

### 3. Intersectional widening — a sub-population that experiences the problem more harshly

**What:** Identify one sub-group within your target user population that experiences the problem differently or more severely. One paragraph.

**Example (for working mothers):** "Underprivileged girls in agricultural laborer families, who shoulder the burden of care and housework when their mothers step out to work, are deprived of opportunities for education."

**Example (for elderly loneliness):** "Elderly domestic workers and informal-sector retirees who have no pension and continue working into ill health, with no formal support structure."

**Why it matters:** Without this, the brief reads as if it only studies privileged users. The intersectional widening is what makes the case study feel like serious research rather than a market segment analysis.

---

### 4. Cultural scripts (Page 1)

**What:** 2–3 cultural norms that compound the problem in your geography. These should be **socialized beliefs**, not biological facts.

**Example (for working mothers in India):**
- Motherhood expected after marriage
- Extended family members typically involved in childcare
- Husbands choosing to stay at home is exceedingly uncommon

**Why it matters:** Page 1's Cultural Scenario section is built from these. Without them, Claude will produce generic cultural commentary or skip the section.

**How to derive yours:** Reflect on your interviews. What did interviewees describe as "the way things are done here"? Those are cultural scripts.

---

### 5. Cultural-scenario pull quote with attribution (Page 1)

**What:** One direct interview quote that captures cultural attribution (i.e., names the *socialized* origin of the cultural script). 1–2 sentences, with the speaker's name and dual identity if available.

**Example:** "From ages they have been seasoned to believe that women's salary is a secondary income and probably doesn't play as much role." — Anika Das, A Developer & A Mother

**Why it matters:** This is the Page 1 pull quote. It must be different from Round 1's pull quote (Round 2 brings new voices). The quote should use language like "seasoned to believe" or "conditioned" or "always been told" — implicating culture, not nature.

---

### 6. Existing service audit — one named service with pros/cons (Page 1)

**What:** Pick one major existing service in your problem space that your team has investigated (ideally via a field visit). Provide:
- Service name (include local-language name if applicable)
- 1–2 sentence description
- 3–5 pros
- 4–6 cons
- Source of your investigation (field visit / interviews / etc.)

**Example:** Anganwadi (आँगनवाडी)
- Description: A government-run childcare facility aiming to create a preschool-like environment for young children.
- Pros: Provide hot meals, Offer immunizations, Community engagement, Play-based education
- Cons: Underfunded, Only 10am–1pm, Poor infrastructure, Unhygienic conditions, Poorly trained workers, Provide minimal services
- Source: Field Visits and Interviews

**Why it matters:** Without an existing-services audit, your later solutions will look ignorant of the landscape. With it, the team has earned the right to propose new work.

**Critical:** Only claim "Field Visits" as a source if a team member actually visited the service. If your team only did desk research on it, say so.

---

### 7. Other named policies and services (Page 1)

**What:** A list of 3–6 other real, named policies or schemes in your problem space, plus the gap claim (what's missing despite these existing).

**Example:** "Breastfeeding stations, NIPCCD, childcare leaves and centers. Schemes like RGNC, PMSMA, and ICDS offer healthcare, nutrition, and education for working mothers and their children." Plus the gap: "Despite these initiatives, there are still gaps in implementation. Even if implemented properly, they aren't accessible to many women because of geographic and cultural constraints."

**Why it matters:** This is the bridge from "here's what exists" to "here's why new work is needed." Use real, verifiable scheme names — do not invent acronyms.

---

### 8. Stakeholder map — 15+ stakeholders across 3 rings (Page 2)

**What:** Categorize 15–25 stakeholders into three rings:
- **CORE (3–5):** the user + immediate family / closest relational unit
- **INVOLVED (10–15):** active touchpoints — services, professionals, institutions
- **INFORMED (5–7):** peripheral observers — colleagues, neighbors, friends, extended community

**Example (working mothers):**
- CORE: Child, Mother, Spouse, Family (Grandparents, Siblings)
- INVOLVED: Daycare, Anganwadi, Workplace, Healthcare, Caretaker, Nanny, Transport, School, Government, Hospitals, Househelp, Gynecologist, Pediatrician, Psychologist
- INFORMED: Employer, Colleagues, Neighbour, Friends, Teachers

**Why it matters:** Page 2's concentric-circle map is built from this. A thin or arbitrary stakeholder list breaks the relational expansion.

---

### 9. Stakeholder quotes — 5 quotes from 5 distinct roles (Page 2)

**What:** 4–6 direct quotes or paraphrases from second-wave interviews with stakeholders from across the ecosystem. Each quote attributed by **role**, not personal name. The roles should triangulate across at least 4 distinct types.

**Example:**
- *Gynecologist:* "Addressing the prevalence of PPD and encouraging affected mothers to seek help can significantly reduce stress, guilt, and anxiety, improving mental health outcomes for both the mother and baby."
- *Pediatrician:* "I've observed that children of employed mothers tend to fall ill more often. Even though high-quality daycares prioritize hygiene, the exposure to a group setting can impact a child's immunity."
- *Spouse:* "Although I aim to divide childcare responsibilities equally with my wife, I encounter difficulties in doing so. For instance, I struggle to take leave from work without having to justify it by stating my wife's unavailability."
- *Child:* "Although I'm glad that my mother works, there were instances when her absence, such as during my performance at the annual day, or when I was sick, left me feeling very upset."
- *Caretaker:* "I face numerous challenges such as managing the needs and safety of multiple children at the same time, adapting to diverse cultural backgrounds, and staying updated with new technology and policies."

**Why it matters:** This is the triangulation move. If all 5 quotes are from family members, or all from medical experts, the page fails. Distribute across at least 4 distinct stakeholder types.

**Critical:** Quote attribution is by ROLE, not name. The Spouse is "Spouse," not "Rajesh Kumar."

---

### 10. Problem-space inventory (Page 3)

**What:** 5–8 primary problem categories, each with 3–8 sub-issues. This is the inventory that will become the radial mind-map.

**Example (working mothers):**
- **Occupational:** Workplace Discrimination, Less work credit, Rejected promotions to take care of child, Difficulty in returning back to work, Lack of Work Policies, Limited Seats at Daycare, lack of flexibility in job, Work Life Balance, Lack of personal vehicle
- **Financial:** Childcare Expenses, Part Time Jobs, Full Time Jobs, Higher stress in public services, Burnout, Pension and Taxes, Unpaid Care Services, Paid Services, Less Leaves
- **Mental Health:** Stress, Regret, Separation Anxiety, Guilt, Post Partum Depression, Lack of self acceptance, Lack of trust, No experts for treatment
- **Child Personal:** Vaccination, Breastfeeding, Missing of Important Events, Missing of Extra Curriculars, Negative Impact on Studies, Disconnect from Mother
- **Physical Health:** Post Pregnancy Complications, Anaemia, Body Insecurity, Hormonal Changes, UTI, Sleep Deprivation, Menstrual Digestion, Breast Soreness, C-section Pain
- **Transport:** Distance, Lack of walking space, Inconvenience with two wheelers, Inadequate Public Facility, Discomfort, Low leg Space

**Why it matters:** Page 3 should look slightly overwhelming. If you provide only 3 categories with 2 sub-issues each, the page won't carry its weight. If you provide 8 categories with 6+ sub-issues each, the density does its job.

**How to derive yours:** Affinity-cluster your interview notes + desk-research findings + field-observation notes. Every distinct issue mentioned in any source becomes a sub-issue. Cluster them into 5–8 primary categories.

---

### 11. Day-in-the-life observations across 4 layers (Page 4)

**What:** Map 6 stages of your user's typical day. For each stage, provide content in all 4 layers:
- Actions (what they DO — concrete, observable)
- Emotions (the emotional state at that stage)
- Thoughts (internal monologue — what's running through their mind)
- Painpoints (specific friction points)

**Example (working mother, "Getting Ready" stage):**
- Actions: Household chores (cleaning, cooking, laundry), Getting kids ready for school
- Emotion: Frustrated (mid-low on curve)
- Thoughts: *focussed on getting the children ready*, worry about whether the *child is ready or not*
- Painpoints: has to manage *too many chores* at one time

**Why it matters:** Without specific content in all 4 layers at all 6 stages, the experience map will have empty cells (revealing under-research) or filler content (generic emotional states like "stressed" without specifics).

**How to derive yours:** Either shadow a real user for a day, or conduct in-depth interviews focused on "walk me through your typical day from waking up to going to sleep." Then bodystorm to fill any remaining gaps.

---

### 12. Bodystorming session record (Page 4 bottom row)

**What:** A brief record of each team member's bodystorming session. Bodystorming = each team member physically simulating the user's day to develop embodied empathy.

**Example:**
- *Avni:* Spent a day pretending to be a working mother — woke at 5am, made breakfast, dropped a stuffed-animal "child" at a friend's place, worked at her usual desk job, picked up the "child," cooked dinner, finalized notes for the next day. Felt the morning rush as the hardest part.
- *Devanshi:* Tried managing household chores while also doing university work for 8 hours. Found it impossible to focus when interruptions kept happening.
- *Urvi:* Followed a 5am-to-midnight schedule with a real toddler from a relative's family for one weekend day. Reported physical exhaustion by 4pm.
- *Arya:* Combined the kitchen + work + childcare simulation. Reported feeling "always behind" by midday.

**Why it matters:** The bodystorming row on Page 4 needs real content. Each team member's wavy line should reflect their actual emotional rhythm during the session. Without real bodystorming, the row is fabricated methodology.

**If your team didn't bodystorm:** Either run a 1-day bodystorming session before finalizing the brief (recommended — it's the single highest-leverage research move for Round 2), or remove the bodystorming row and adjust Page 4's caption to not claim it.

---

### 13. Two contrasting personas (Page 5)

**What:** Two personas on a deliberate contrast axis. For each, provide:
- Photo concept (or character description) + Name + Role
- Age, Kid(s) + age(s), Family Type, Childcare arrangement, Working Hours
- Goals (3–6 bullets)
- Motivations (3–5 bullets)
- Painpoints and Frustrations (a list of 8–12, with notes on which are most severe — these will become the word-cloud sizing)

**Contrast axis options:**
- Organized vs. unorganized sector (white-collar vs. blue-collar)
- Urban vs. rural
- Nuclear vs. joint family
- First-time vs. experienced
- High-resource vs. low-resource
- Native to context vs. migrant

**Example contrast (working mothers):**

*Persona A — Chitra Tiwari, Software Engineer*
- 33, 1 kid (1 year old), Nuclear family (Husband + kid), Daycare for childcare, 10am–6pm working hours
- Goals: Work for herself, Child's well-being, Spend proper time with child, Efficiently manage work and home, Time for self
- Motivations: Career satisfaction, Child's growth, Support from husband
- Top painpoints: Child falling ill, Lack of Proper Workpolicies, Guilt, Improper Work Life Balance

*Persona B — Rashmi Joshi, Construction Worker*
- 26, 2 kids (6 months + 4 years), Joint family (Husband + Parents + 2 kids), Parents care for kids, 8am–6pm working hours
- Goals: To earn a living, Provide for family, Children's well-being, Take care of family, Ensure better future for kids
- Motivations: Providing for family, Child's growth, Social mobility, Health of child and parents, Getting basic necessities
- Top painpoints: Lack of Nutrition, Hygiene, Physical Exhaustion, Improper Work Life Balance

**Why it matters:** Two personas that are variations of each other defeat the purpose of having two. The contrast axis must reveal something meaningful about how the problem manifests across your population.

**Test for whether the contrast works:** If Persona A's #1 goal is "self-actualization" and Persona B's #1 goal is "survival," you've found a strong axis. If both lists are interchangeable, redesign.

### 14. Three invalidated assumptions with evidence (Page 5)

**What:** 3 genuine beliefs your team held entering this round that the research disproved. Each pair: the assumption stated as a remembered belief, the reality stated with evidence.

**Example:**
1. Assumption: "Daycares hinder child development."
   Reality: Good daycare centres **promote socialisation, independence & maturity.** (Source: Pediatrician interviews, field visits to two daycares.)

2. Assumption: "Women need longer maternity leaves."
   Reality: Even **four weeks are enough** for physical recovery, and returning to work soon would help ease separation anxiety. (Source: Gynecologist interview, two working-mother interviews.)

3. Assumption: "Office work is the hardest part."
   Reality: The **most challenging is the 6–9am rush**, balancing multiple responsibilities while feeling the pressure of being late. (Source: Bodystorming session + 4 mother interviews.)

**Why it matters:** This is the intellectual humility move. Without it, the brief reads as if the team confirmed all their priors — which is suspicious and rare in good research.

**Critical:** The assumptions must be *genuine*. If you can't think of 3 assumptions your team had that turned out wrong, either:
- You haven't done enough research yet (most likely)
- You're not noticing your assumptions being invalidated as it happens (also likely — keep a "what surprised us this week" doc during research)
- The research really did confirm all your priors (rare — try harder to surface what you missed)

Strawman examples to avoid:
- "We thought working mothers don't love their children." (No one believed this)
- "We thought childcare was easy." (No one believed this)
- "We thought there were no policies for working women." (Factually invalidated, not assumption-invalidated)

---

### 15. Closing scope re-affirmation

**What:** 2–4 sentences honoring a population that's outside your scope (but valuable), then re-stating your focus.

**Example:** "During this phase, we developed an appreciation for the important work performed by mothers at home, including their often-unrecognized unpaid labor. While we recognize that every mother is a working mother, our design process will primarily center on employed mothers."

**Why it matters:** This is the Round 2 equivalent of the scope disclaimer on Round 1's emotional arc. Without it, Page 5 ends abruptly.

## High-value optional inputs

### 16. Discoveries — 2–3 statistics that surfaced during Round 2 research

**What:** 2–3 statistics from your deeper research (not from Round 1) with large-number callouts and 1-sentence explanations + sources.

**Example:**
- 50% of working mothers noted **transportation to work** as a concern post-motherhood (Source: Field Research + Interviews)
- 86% experienced one or more symptoms of **Postpartum Depression** (Source: Gynecologist interview + secondary data)

**Why it matters:** These are the Discoveries section on Page 5. Without them, that section will use generic or Round 1 stats — defeating the "we discovered new things this round" claim.

---

### 17. Visual style continuity notes

**What:** Any visual choices to maintain from Round 1, or new visual directions for Round 2.

**Why it matters:** Round 2 should feel like the same team's continued work. If you switched designers between rounds or made palette changes, note them.

---

### 18. Field visit notes

**What:** Notes from any field visits — especially relevant to the Existing Services audit on Page 1.

**Why it matters:** Specific field-visit observations (specific times of day, specific staff behaviors, specific infrastructure conditions) make the pros/cons audit credible.

## How to gather these inputs

**For the filtration funnel stages:** Walk through your user's typical life timeline from earliest to latest. Identify the major transitions where opportunity or agency narrows. Those are your filtration stages.

**For the cultural scripts:** Re-read your Round 1 + new interview transcripts looking for phrases like "that's just how it is here," "people expect," "we're told to," "it's normal that…" Those are cultural scripts.

**For the stakeholder map:** Brainstorm with your team — for one full hour — every person, role, or institution that touches your target user's life. Then cluster by closeness (CORE / INVOLVED / INFORMED).

**For the stakeholder quotes:** Conduct second-wave interviews with at least 4 distinct stakeholder types. If you only interviewed users in Round 1, Round 2 is your chance to talk to the people *around* the user.

**For the problem-space inventory:** Sticky-note exercise. Write every distinct problem you've heard mentioned across all your research on its own sticky note. Cluster them. The clusters become your branches.

**For the day-in-the-life:** Ask your interviewees, "Walk me through a typical day from when you wake up to when you go to sleep." Take granular notes.

**For the bodystorming:** Block 1 full day per team member. Each person picks a piece of the user's life to simulate. Take notes hourly on what felt hard.

**For the personas:** Pick a contrast axis from the list. Then ask: "Who in my interview pool best represents the high end? The low end?" Build personas as composites of those subgroups.

**For invalidated assumptions:** Keep a "what surprised us this week" doc throughout your research. By Round 2, you should have 5–10 surprises. Pick 3 that count as assumption-invalidations.

## Common input mistakes

1. **Repeating Round 1 content.** Your filtration funnel stages, cultural scripts, problem-space inventory, and personas should be *new content* derived from your Round 2 research — not recycled Round 1 phrases.

2. **Skipping the bodystorming.** Bodystorming is the single highest-leverage activity for a credible Round 2. If your team didn't bodystorm, do a 1-day session before invoking the skill.

3. **Stakeholder quotes from interviews you didn't do.** Don't invent quotes. If you only interviewed mothers in Round 1, talk to 4 more stakeholder types (Spouse, Child, Caretaker, Pediatrician, etc.) before invoking this skill.

4. **Stakeholder quotes attributed by name instead of role.** Round 2 quotes use ROLE labels (Gynecologist, Spouse, Child) — not personal names. The one exception is the Page 1 cultural pull quote, which retains the Round 1 dual-identity full-name convention.

5. **Two personas that are variations of each other.** If you can swap their painpoint lists and barely notice, the contrast axis is too weak. Pick a sharper axis (class / geography / family structure / resource level).

6. **Strawman invalidated assumptions.** Don't invent obviously-wrong beliefs to disprove. The assumptions must be things a reasonable person actually held.

7. **Field-visit attribution without a field visit.** If the team didn't actually visit an Anganwadi (or equivalent), don't cite "Field Visits" as a source. Be honest about what research method generated each finding.

8. **Empty cells in the experience map.** If you can't fill in Thoughts and Painpoints for the "Going to Sleep" stage, that's a research gap — go interview more deeply rather than leaving cells empty or filling them with generic content.

9. **Solution-leakage in problem-space inventory.** The mind-map is for *problems*, not *solution opportunities*. "Build a childcare scheduling app" doesn't belong on Page 3.

10. **Persona facts that don't match the contrast axis claim.** If you say "unorganized sector construction worker, joint family" but list their working hours as 10am-6pm, those facts don't match the population. Be plausible.

---

## What happens at each input quality level

- **Mandatory inputs only:** You get a competent draft that may struggle on the Empathic Granularity dimension (if quotes or experience-map content is thin) and on Persona Contrast & Humility (if the personas don't sharply contrast).

- **Mandatory + half the optional inputs:** Strong draft that mostly hits Scoring Benchmark targets. Some sections feel more "yours" than others.

- **All inputs filled in:** Portfolio-grade draft where every page reflects your team's specific Round 2 research. The Scoring Benchmark targets should all be met on the first pass.

The 1–2 weeks of fieldwork + 1–2 hours of filling out this template is the highest-leverage investment in Round 2's final quality. Skipping the fieldwork is the single biggest reason Round 2 briefs fail in portfolio reviews.
