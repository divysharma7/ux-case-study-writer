# Input Checklist for the Solution-Design Brief Case Study Skill (Round 4)

This document specifies what the user needs to provide for the Round 4 skill to produce a portfolio-grade final brief. Round 4 has the most demanding input requirements of the four rounds — it depends on completed Rounds 1, 2, and 3, plus the team's actual ideation work (How-to questions), actual co-creation sessions, actual A/B testing data, an actually-designed solution with named features and service flow, and stress-tested FAQs. **Without these inputs, the skill will either ask for them, leave clearly-flagged gaps, or produce a brief that fails the Scoring Benchmark on Research-to-Solution Traceability and Solution Concreteness.**

There are two tiers of inputs:

- **Mandatory inputs** — without these, the skill cannot produce a credible Round 4. Do not skip these.
- **High-value optional inputs** — each significantly improves a specific section.

A fillable template is at the bottom. The recommended workflow: complete Rounds 1, 2, and 3 first, then do the actual ideation + design + testing work over 2-3 weeks, then fill out the template, paste it alongside all prior briefs, and invoke the skill.

---

## Why this matters

Round 4 is where the team **presents the result of months of work**. The original Team Dua Round 4 brief works because:

- They actually did ideation (the How-to questions cluster shows real divergent thinking)
- They actually did co-creation with 5 mothers + their children
- They actually did A/B testing with 70 mothers using posters with storyboards
- They actually designed a coherent solution (Togit) with 5 specified features, a 9-step service flow, and 7 anticipated FAQs
- They actually thought about scalability across 3 distinct growth axes
- They actually returned to Round 1's wellbeing framework to show fulfilment

Without analogous work in your project, Claude has two options: ask you to do the work first, or fabricate it. Fabrication on Round 4 is the highest-risk fabrication of any round because the solution itself is the deliverable — if the solution doesn't exist, there is no Round 4.

**Treat this checklist as a 2-3 week investment between Round 3 and Round 4.** This typically includes: 1 week of ideation (generating How-to questions, sketching 3 concepts), 1 week of co-creation (sessions with 4-6 users), 1 week of A/B testing (with a larger N if accessible).

---

## Continuity requirements

Before filling out the Round 4 checklist, you should have:

**From Round 1:**
- HMW question
- Wellbeing framework (or equivalent) with focus dimensions named
- Target user, geography, team

**From Round 2:**
- A hero stat that captures the magnitude of the problem (e.g., "73% of new mothers…")
- Problem mapping including the issues your solution will address

**From Round 3:**
- The chosen problem area
- The Need Statement (verbatim — Round 4 will reproduce this)
- The 3 refined sub-HMWs (these inform the How-to questions)
- The market data + business viability case

If any of these are missing, return to the prior skills first.

## Mandatory inputs

### 1. Project journey recap (Page 1 Background)

**What:** A single paragraph (2-3 sentences) in past tense, looking back across the whole project.

**Example:** "Throughout the course of this challenge, our objective was to thoroughly examine the various obstacles that working mothers encounter in attaining their career objectives and the impact it has on their overall well-being."

**Why it matters:** This is the loop-opening move. Without it, Round 4 doesn't establish itself as a retrospective.

---

### 2. 5 macro causes for the Cause and Effect Tree

**What:** 5 high-level causes pulled from Rounds 1 and 2 that converge on the chosen problem area. Each cause is a short noun phrase (4-10 words). One of them (typically the last one) is the cause most directly connected to the chosen problem and will branch into sub-causes.

**Example (working mothers):**
1. Underrepresentation of women in senior job roles
2. High dropout rates during the initial phases of their careers
3. Challenges in achieving a harmonious work-life balance
4. Heightened familial obligations after marriage and pregnancy
5. **Insufficient facilities to raise a child** ← this one branches

**Why it matters:** This is the synthesis of all prior rounds in one diagram. Without it, Round 4 doesn't close the loop on the macro problem.

**Common mistake:** Listing causes that don't appear in Rounds 1 or 2. The causes must be synthesized from prior research, not invented.

---

### 3. Sub-causes off the focused cause

**What:** 5-8 sub-causes that branch off the cause most connected to your chosen problem area. These should be pulled from Round 3's pain points or problem space.

**Example (off "Insufficient facilities to raise a child"):** Absence of trust & reliability, Transport issues, Lack of a homely environment, Lack of individual attention, Lack of value-driven activities, Unaffordable facilities, Lack of transparency, Under-developed childcare curriculum.

**Why it matters:** Sub-causes show the specific problems your solution will address. They become the de facto requirements list.

---

### 4. Hero stat to reactivate from prior rounds

**What:** Pick one powerful statistic from Rounds 1, 2, or 3 to reproduce on Page 1. Verbatim — same number, same caption, same source.

**Example:** "73% of new mothers with children less than 6yrs old, primarily attributed their unemployment to childcare responsibilities. Source: 'Creating a Gender-Equitable Workforce in India' by FSG GLOW."

**Why it matters:** The stat reactivation reminds the reader why this work matters before the solution is revealed.

---

### 5. Round 3 Need Statement verbatim

**What:** Copy your Round 3 Need Statement word-for-word.

**Why it matters:** The Need Statement is the contract Round 4 is fulfilling. It must be reproduced exactly so the reader can verify the solution addresses the need.

**Common mistake:** Paraphrasing or rewriting the Need Statement. Don't — use exact wording.

---

### 6. 12+ How-to questions covering multiple angles

**What:** A cluster of 12-15+ "How to..." questions generated during your ideation sessions. Each is 5-15 words. Together they should span across multiple solution-space angles (emotional, operational, regulatory, cultural, safety, community, healthcare, stakeholder coordination).

**Example angles:**
- Emotional barriers: "How to bring out mothers from the deep guilt of leaving their child at a childcare facility?"
- Practical operations: "How to ensure safe transportation between homes and the daycare centre?"
- Regulatory/trust: "How to involve government in this process to build trust and reliability?"
- Awareness/culture: "How to remove the stigma related to daycare?"
- Safety: "How to find a trustworthy caretaker for the children?"
- Community: "How to help new mothers build a community?"
- Healthcare: "How to ensure proper healthcare and nutrition of the child?"
- Stakeholder coordination: "How to make children and parents feel comfortable and welcomed in the childcare facility?"

**Why it matters:** This cluster shows the team's ideation breadth before convergence. Without it, the eventual solution appears arbitrary.

**How to gather:** Run an actual ideation session (1-2 hours). Use the "How might we..." or "How to..." prompt against each pain point from Round 3. Aim for 30+ questions during the session, then narrow to your best 12-15 for the brief.

---

### 7. Current Scenario → Challenges → Vision triple

**What:** Three short phrases (3-6 words each) that compress the project's argument into one horizontal row.

**Example:**
- Current Scenario: "Declining female workforce participation"
- Challenges: "Insufficient childcare support"
- Vision: "Provide a safe and reliable childcare service"

**Why it matters:** This triple is the one-line argument structure of the entire project.

---

### 8. Co-creation methodology details

**What:**
- Number of users + linked parties involved in co-creation
- How the co-creation was conducted (e.g., workshop, home visits, group sessions)
- What was created (e.g., concept sketches, storyboards, journey maps)

**Example:** "We engaged 5 employed mothers and their children to co-create with us. We ran 2-hour home-based sessions where mothers and children built sticky-note storyboards of their ideal childcare day. Three concept directions emerged from these sessions."

**Why it matters:** Without this, the validation stats below are unsupported.

**Critical:** If the team did not actually do co-creation, do not invent it. Acknowledge what was actually done (interviews? secondary research? team brainstorming?) and let Claude reframe.

---

### 9. A/B testing methodology details

**What:**
- Number of testing participants
- Method (A/B testing, concept comparison, prototype testing, etc.)
- Materials used (posters, storyboards, prototypes, key feature lists)

**Example:** "We involved 70 mothers using the A/B testing method to select the most favorable concept. Each mother saw a poster featuring storyboards and key service features for each of our 3 concept directions, and selected their preferred concept along with explanations."

**Why it matters:** This is the validation-step methodology that backs the three hero stats.

**Critical:** Use the actual N. If the team tested with 12 mothers, report 12, not 70.

---

### 10. 3 hero validation statistics

**What:** Three percentage-based stats from your testing, each measuring a different success dimension:
- Approval / preference (X% approved or chose this concept)
- Trust / quality perception (X% said it's trustworthy / safe / reliable)
- Outcome impact (X% said it would deliver a specific outcome — reduce stress, improve their day, etc.)

**Example:**
- 74% of our users approved of our solution
- 77% of mothers said it's trustworthy
- 68% said this would significantly reduce stress

**Why it matters:** Three stats from three angles is more credible than one stat from one angle. The trio shows the solution was validated across multiple dimensions.

**Critical:** Use real numbers from real testing. Do not fabricate.

---

### 11. Challenges Conquered (sidebar)

**What:** 3-5 short phrases (6-15 words each) naming research-execution challenges the team encountered — things that were hard about *doing the research itself*, not the problem's substance.

**Example:**
- Securing permission to interact with children in a daycare setting
- Involving mothers in the co-creation activities due to their busy schedules
- Executing user testing with unacquainted participants
- Building a rapport with children to gain valuable insights

**Why it matters:** This is the research-humility move that makes the validation stats credible.

---

### 12. Solution name with built-in meaning

**What:** A name for your solution where the name itself embodies the core mechanic or value proposition. Include the expanded meaning of the name.

**Examples:**
- "Togit (Together in Time)" — intergenerational care
- "Sanjeevini (Daily Companion)" — could embody elderly companionship
- "Setu (Bridge)" — could embody inter-generational bridging

**Why it matters:** A name with built-in positioning telegraphs the concept before any explanation.

**How to derive yours:** Workshop with the team for 30-60 minutes. Consider:
- Sanskrit / regional language words that capture the core mechanic
- Compound words from English (e.g., "Together in Time" → "Togit")
- Names of mythological / cultural figures relevant to the concept
- Action-implication words ("Bridge," "Anchor," "Hearth")

**Common mistake:** Generic English brand names that don't carry meaning ("ChildCare Pro," "BetterDay"). The name should do positioning work.

---

### 13. Concept description paragraph (3-5 sentences)

**What:** A tight paragraph that packs in:
- Core mechanic — what fundamentally novel thing the service does
- Economic model — how it stays affordable / sustains itself
- Unit economics — specific ratios, capacities, operational constants
- Emotional outcome — what the user feels

**Example:** "Togit (Together in Time) provides an affordable and unique intergenerational childcare ecosystem by repurposing existing spaces and partnering with local communities. Trained caretakers and elderly volunteers offer personalized care with a 2-5 child per caretaker ratio, fostering trust and growth while combating loneliness. Lesser staff reduces costs, creating a homely environment."

---

### 14. Primary stakeholders (3-4 named)

**What:** The 3-4 populations your service is designed for — not just the original Round 1 user, but everyone the service serves.

**Example:** Elderly, Mother, Children

**Why it matters:** Multi-stakeholder framing implicitly argues that the solution creates value for multiple parties, which is often what makes the unit economics work.

---

### 15. 5 Key Features with mechanic + emotional outcome paragraphs

**What:** For each of 5 features:
- Short feature name (1-3 words, concrete, domain-specific)
- 2-4 sentence paragraph following the mechanic → emotional outcome pattern
- Bolded key phrases inside the paragraph

**Feature mix should include:**
1. The core differentiator (what makes this unique)
2. The convenience play (how it removes user friction)
3. The cost-control play (how it stays affordable)
4. The digital layer (app or tech component)
5. The first-touch experience (onboarding)

**Example feature paragraph:**
> "**Intergenerational Care** — Each child is paired with an elderly member of the society who volunteers in addition to professional caretakers, to offer personalized attention. This imbues a home-like atmosphere and fosters trust in mothers and values such as respect and empathy in children. It facilitates reciprocal learning experiences while addressing elderly loneliness, promoting happiness and self-esteem and also results in cost savings from reduced staffing needs."

**For the App feature:** include 3-7 specific app features as a sub-bullet list, organized by user role.

---

### 16. Service Flow (9-12 illustrated steps)

**What:** A sequence of 9-12 steps showing the service blueprint. Each step has a short action-oriented label.

**Step mix should include:**
- Setup steps (one-time): Set Up, Spread Awareness, Enrolment
- Onboarding steps (per-user): Onboarding Kit provided, Induction
- Daily operation steps (recurring): Drop off, Activities, Meal Time, Pick Up
- Communication steps (recurring): Updates through App
- Systemic outcome step (long-term): the emergent benefit

**Example:**
1. Set Up
2. Spread Awareness
3. Enrolment of mothers and elderly
4. Onboarding Kit provided to Mothers and Children
5. Drop off Child
6. Induction of the Elderly
7. Playful activities involving Kids, Caretakers and Elderly Buddies
8. Regular Child Updates to mothers through App
9. Meal Time with Caretakers, Kids and Elderly
10. Pick Up by Mother
11. Development of an inclusive and happy society ← the emergent outcome

**Critical:** the final step should be a systemic outcome step (the long-term societal benefit), not an operational close.

---

### 17. 6-8 substantive FAQs with mechanism-explaining answers

**What:** For each FAQ:
- A substantive critical question (not softball)
- A 2-4 sentence answer explaining the mechanism, not just policy
- Bolded key phrases in the answer

**Pick from these 7 question categories** (cover at least 5):
1. The host community's WIIFM
2. The volunteer / staff recruitment question
3. The edge case (what if X goes wrong?)
4. The showstopper question (single most important risk)
5. The inclusivity question (special needs / circumstances)
6. The staffing / training question
7. The operational availability question

**Example FAQ:**
> **Q. How are you ensuring child safety?**
> We provide **live CCTV surveillance with facial detection technology** to ensure privacy and safety. As we operate within residential societies, the safety aspect is well taken care of, supplemented by **strict access rules**. The **tap-in ID cards** ensure the people entering the centre are registered and directly associated with us. Additionally, we conduct **comprehensive background checks** on our workers and elderly members before they join our team, ensuring a trustworthy environment.

**Critical:** answers must explain *mechanism* (specific features, processes, technologies), not just *policy* ("we ensure safety").

---

### 18. 12-15 benefits for the Word Cloud

**What:** A list of 12-15 short benefit phrases (2-4 words each) for the Page 4 word cloud. For each, note relative importance/weight (which ones should be largest/most prominent).

**Example:** Enhanced Trust (large), Reduced Stress (large), Homely Feeling (large), Individual Attention (large), Familiar Environment (medium), Easy Pick Up and Drop Off (medium), Low Cost (medium), Holistic Child Development (medium), Enhanced Work Efficiency (medium), Increased Security (medium), Flexible Service hours (small), Enhance Cultural Connection (small), Communal Support (small).

**Critical:** each benefit must trace back to a specific feature or mechanic. If you can't explain *why* this benefit comes from the solution, cut it.

---

### 19. Wellbeing framework return (Page 4)

**What:** Confirmation of the framework from Round 1 (typically 6 wellbeing dimensions: Physical, Emotional, Environmental, Social, Financial, Occupational) + a 4-5 sentence paragraph naming which dimensions the solution actually addresses, with bolded dimension names.

**Example:** "Togit improves women's **emotional and occupational well-being** through quality childcare, reducing parental isolation and overwhelm. We foster **social cohesion** for a happier society across generations. Our focus on an optimal curriculum ensures children's **physical well-being**, while a sustainable collaborative economy positively impacts the **environment**."

**Why it matters:** This is the most important loop-closing move in the brief. Without it, the team's Round 1 commitment is unfulfilled.

**Critical:** Don't claim the solution addresses all 6 dimensions equally if Round 1 only committed to 2. Modest claims preserve credibility.

---

### 20. 5 Impact statements (each tied to a feature)

**What:** For each of 5 impact statements:
- A headline in the format "[Category]: [Specific framing]"
- A 2-3 sentence paragraph with bolded key phrases
- Confirmation of which feature(s) backs the claim

**The 5 should ripple outward:**
1. Primary beneficiary emotional impact
2. Structural / equity impact
3. Community impact
4. Cultural / awareness impact
5. Environmental / sustainability impact

**Example:**
> **Promoting a Greener Future: Repurposing Spaces, Managing Resources**
> By **transforming vacant areas into community hubs** and **using resources equitably through recycling and sharing**, we reduce waste, enhance efficiency, and promote sustainability. These practices not only benefit the environment but also foster social well-being and resilience.
> (Backed by: Space Modularity feature, society-embedded location)

---

### 21. 3 Scalability directions at distinct growth axes

**What:** Three short paragraphs each naming a different growth axis. The three axes should NOT be variations of the same expansion.

**Example axes:**
- Supply-chain / resource axis: managing surplus resources, circular economy
- Existing-market upgrade axis: repurposing existing daycares to enhance quality
- New-venue partnership axis: schools, workplaces, business hubs

**Why it matters:** Three distinct axes signal multiple growth paths. One axis signals premature commitment.

---

### 22. Closing emotional statement

**What:** A paragraph (3-5 sentences) combining:
- Backward acknowledgment (what the team felt or learned during the project)
- Forward commitment (what the team will do, using identity language with the team name as subject)

**Template:**
> "Throughout this challenge, our team developed **[emotional disposition]** for all the [USERS], recognizing **[sacrifices/difficulties]**. Our vision is to **[forward verb]** this journey for these [USERS] by becoming **[team's role]** they need to **[outcome]**, ensuring that **[the core promise]**. **Team [Name] is wholeheartedly dedicated to make this vision a reality.**"

**Critical:** The closing line must name the team as the subject of a forward commitment. Not "It is our hope…" — instead "Team X is dedicated…"

---

### 23. Team credit line (consistent with prior rounds)

**What:** "Team [Name]: [Member 1], [Member 2], [Member 3], [Member 4]"

---

## High-value optional inputs

### 24. Co-creation session notes

**What:** Brief notes from each co-creation session — what concepts emerged, which were rejected, which the team converged on.

**Why it matters:** Adds authenticity to the methodology framing.

---

### 25. A/B testing results breakdown

**What:** More granular testing data — e.g., qualitative themes from open-ended responses, demographic breakdowns of preferences.

**Why it matters:** Strengthens the validation evidence.

---

### 26. Visual style notes for the isometric illustration

**What:** Specific notes on the visual context the service operates in (e.g., apartment complex with specific cultural visual cues, urban setting with specific identifiable infrastructure).

**Why it matters:** The isometric illustration on Page 2 grounds the solution in its real-world context.

---

### 27. References to specific personas from Round 2

**What:** Note which Round 2 personas the solution serves, and how specifically. E.g., "The Space Modularity feature directly addresses Chitra Tiwari's affordability concern; the Intergenerational Care feature directly addresses Rashmi Joshi's joint-family-style support need."

**Why it matters:** Strengthens Research-to-Solution Traceability.

---

## How to gather these inputs

**For the How-to questions:** Block 2 hours with the team. Use each pain point from Round 3 as a prompt. Generate 30+ questions; narrow to 15.

**For co-creation:** Block 2-3 weeks for sessions with 4-6 users + their linked parties. Use sticky-note storyboarding, journey-mapping, or sketch-based methods. Capture concepts as they emerge.

**For A/B testing:** Develop 2-3 concept directions as posters with storyboards. Recruit 30-70 users for testing. Ask them to choose between concepts and explain why. Capture quantitative preferences AND qualitative explanations.

**For the solution name:** Workshop with the team for an hour. Consider Sanskrit / regional words, compound English words, mythological references, action verbs.

**For features and service flow:** Sketch the solution in detail. Map out 5 distinct features and a 9-12 step service flow before drafting prose.

**For FAQs:** Show your concept to 3-5 outsiders (not in the project) and ask "What would worry you about this?" Their concerns become your FAQs.

**For impact + scalability:** Reflect on the project's potential ripples. Map each potential impact back to a feature. Identify 3 distinct growth axes from the unit economics.

## Common input mistakes

1. **Fabricating validation stats.** You bring 74%/77%/68% to Claude without having done actual testing. Fix: report the actual N and actual results. If testing was small-scale, say so.

2. **Solution name without meaning.** "ChildCare Pro" or "BetterDay." Fix: invest 30-60 minutes in naming. The name should embody the mechanic.

3. **Macro causes don't appear in prior rounds.** The cause-and-effect tree introduces new content. Fix: every cause must be findable in Rounds 1 or 2.

4. **Features as bullet points without mechanic paragraphs.** Fix: each feature needs 2-4 sentences mixing mechanic and emotional outcome.

5. **Service flow ends on operational step.** "Pick Up by Mother" is the last step. Fix: end on emergent systemic outcome ("Development of inclusive and happy society" or analogous).

6. **Softball FAQs.** "What is [product name]?" Fix: FAQs must be substantive critical questions a skeptical evaluator would actually ask.

7. **FAQ answers are policy, not mechanism.** "We ensure safety." Fix: name specific features, processes, technologies that deliver the safety.

8. **Benefits that don't trace to features.** Word cloud includes "Joy" but no feature delivers joy. Fix: every benefit must trace back to a specific feature.

9. **Wellbeing claims expand beyond Round 1's commitment.** Fix: be honest about which dimensions the solution actually targets.

10. **Impact statements without feature traceability.** Fix: each impact must trace to a specific feature.

11. **Scalability directions all on one axis.** All three are venue expansions. Fix: pick three distinct growth axes.

12. **Closing statement is corporate or empty.** Fix: combine backward acknowledgment + forward commitment + team name as subject.

13. **Marketing language slips in.** "Revolutionary," "game-changing," "next-generation." Fix: cut these. The brief presents, doesn't pitch.

---

## What happens at each input quality level

- **Mandatory inputs only:** Competent draft that may struggle on Validation & Impact Sharpness (if testing data is thin) and Solution Concreteness (if feature paragraphs are thin).

- **Mandatory + half the optional inputs:** Strong draft mostly hitting Scoring Benchmark targets.

- **All inputs filled in:** Portfolio-grade draft where every page reflects your team's specific Round 4 work. Scoring Benchmark targets should be met on the first pass.

The 2-3 weeks of ideation + co-creation + A/B testing is the highest-leverage investment for Round 4. Round 4 cannot be faked — it presents what the team has actually built.
