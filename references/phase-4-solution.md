# Solution-Design Brief Case Study (Team Dua style, Round 4)

This skill produces a 4-page final-round solution brief in the visual and narrative style of the "Team Dua" working-mothers case study, Round 4. It is the **Deliver** / **Solution Presentation** phase of a service-design project — the deliverable that takes the team from "we have committed to this problem area with a refined need statement" (Round 3's output) to "here is our solution, here's how it works, here's the evidence it works, and here's the impact + scale we believe it will achieve."

It is the fourth in the paired skill series:
1. `discovery-brief-case-study` (Round 1)
2. `empathy-deepening-brief-case-study` (Round 2)
3. `problem-convergence-brief-case-study` (Round 3)
4. `solution-design-brief-case-study` (Round 4, this skill)

The output is a **content + layout specification**. Claude writes the project recap, cause-and-effect synthesis, How-to questions cluster, validation methodology + stats, solution concept with name, key features, service flow steps, FAQs, benefits word cloud content, wellbeing framework re-link, impact statements, scalability directions, and the closing commitment statement, and specifies all visual elements needed.

---

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the user asks for:

- A Round 4 / Final-Round / Deliver-phase case study brief
- A solution-presentation deliverable
- A service blueprint with illustrated steps
- A key-features specification for a designed service
- A critique-anticipating FAQ section
- A validation-and-testing summary with hero stats
- A solution name with built-in positioning
- A 5-layer impact case + scalability projection grounded in the solution

**Do not use this skill if Rounds 1, 2, and 3 haven't been done.** Round 4 is fundamentally a loop-closing deliverable — it cannot do its job without all three prior rounds to close back to. If prior rounds are missing, suggest the user complete them first using the corresponding skills.

---

## The core mental model: The Loop

Round 1 was the **Funnel** — narrowing global context to one HMW.
Round 2 was the **Expansion** — opening that HMW into a full problem ecosystem.
Round 3 was the **Converge → Commit → Refine** arc — picking one focus and refining the question.
Round 4 is **The Loop** — closing back to every prior round while presenting the new solution.

The 10-move arc of Round 4:

1. **Recap the journey** — backwards-looking opening paragraph naming the project's overall objective
2. **Diagram the causal chain** — a root-cause tree that synthesizes the macro problems from Rounds 1-2 and connects them to the chosen focus from Round 3
3. **Reactivate prior anchors** — reproduce the most powerful hero statistic from earlier rounds AND the Round 3 Need Statement verbatim
4. **Show ideation breadth** — a cluster of 12+ How-to questions demonstrating divergent thinking before convergence on one solution
5. **Show the gap** — a three-beat Current Scenario → Challenges → Vision triple compressing the entire project's argument into one row
6. **Show validation** — name the dual research methods (co-creation with a small N + A/B testing with a larger N) and present 3 hero validation stats
7. **Show research humility** — a Challenges Conquered sidebar naming the friction the team encountered while doing the research
8. **Reveal the named solution** — a brand name with built-in positioning, plus a tight concept description packing mechanic + economics + emotional outcome
9. **Detail the solution** — 5 key features (each with icon + name + paragraph mixing mechanic and emotional outcome) + a service blueprint with 9-11 illustrated steps
10. **Close the loop and look forward** — FAQs anticipating critical objections, a benefits word cloud, a return to Round 1's wellbeing framework showing the solution fulfils those commitments, 5 impact statements ripping outward, 3 scalability directions, and a closing emotional statement combining backward acknowledgment with forward commitment

The structural argument the brief makes is: "Here is our solution. It connects directly to every research finding across the prior rounds. We tested it. It works. Here's the impact it will create and the ways it can scale."

If your draft doesn't visibly close back to prior rounds at multiple points, the round isn't doing its job. The defining move of Round 4 is *loop closure*.

## Continuity with Rounds 1, 2, and 3

Before drafting, verify the following carry over cleanly:

- **The Round 1 HMW** is referenced (typically the Round 3 Need Statement is reproduced; the HMW is implied via the Cause-and-Effect tree)
- **The Round 3 Need Statement is reproduced verbatim** in a callout on Page 1
- **The chosen problem area from Round 3** is what the solution addresses
- **The wellbeing framework from Round 1** is returned to on Page 4 — showing the solution fulfils the team's original commitments
- **A hero stat from Rounds 1-2** is reactivated on Page 1
- **The voice, palette, visual style, team name, and team members** are unchanged

Round 4 is loop-closure. Any pivot in scope, user, or framework breaks the project's coherence and disqualifies this round from being a true Round 4.

## Section-by-section writing playbook

### Page 1, Section 1: Background

**Function:** A backwards-looking recap of the project's overall objective. Sets the tone of *completion* rather than *exploration*.

**Length:** 1 short paragraph (2-3 sentences).

**Structure:**
"Throughout the course of this challenge, our objective was to thoroughly examine the various **obstacles that [USER GROUP] encounter** in attaining their **[GOAL]** and the impact it has on their **overall well-being**."

This single sentence does heavy work: names the time scope (the whole challenge), the user group, their attempted goal, and the wellbeing framing. It mirrors the opening of Round 1's Introduction but in past tense — the project is now seen retrospectively.

### Page 1, Section 2: Cause and Effect Analysis

**Function:** A root-cause tree that synthesizes prior rounds' research into one diagram. Macro causes branch into the specific causes that converge on the chosen problem area.

**Length:** Diagrammatic — 5 macro causes + 5-8 sub-causes branching off one of them + 1 hero stat + 1 need statement reprise.

**Structure:**
- **5 macro causes**, each with a custom icon. Examples for the original:
  1. Underrepresentation of women in senior job roles
  2. High dropout rates during initial career phases
  3. Challenges in achieving a harmonious work-life balance
  4. Heightened familial obligations after marriage and pregnancy
  5. **Insufficient facilities to raise a child** ← this one branches further

- The 5 macro causes should be **pulled from Rounds 1 and 2's broader research**. They represent the original surface-level problems. They're not new — they're a synthesis of what's already been documented.

- **The 5th cause** (the one closest to the chosen problem area) **branches into sub-causes**. For the original, "Insufficient facilities to raise a child" branched into 8 sub-causes: Absence of trust & reliability, Transport issues, Lack of homely environment, Lack of individual attention, Lack of value-driven activities, Unaffordable facilities, Lack of transparency, Under-developed childcare curriculum.

- **The sub-causes should be pulled from Round 3's pain-points** — they're the specific failures the chosen problem area has, which the solution will address.

- **Hero stat reprise:** A large numeric callout reproducing one of the most powerful stats from Rounds 1-3 (typically Round 2's data on the chosen problem area). Example: "**73%** of new mothers with children less than 6yrs old, primarily attributed their unemployment to childcare responsibilities. Source: 'Creating a Gender-Equitable Workforce in India' by FSG GLOW."

- **Need statement reprise:** The full Round 3 Need Statement reproduced verbatim in a callout/quote bubble with oversized quotation marks.

The diagram structurally argues: "Here are all the macro problems that converged on our chosen focus area. Here are the specific sub-causes within that area. Here is the stat that quantifies why this matters. Here is the need statement that scopes what we'll address."

### Page 1, Section 3: Asking the Right Questions? (How-to Questions Cluster)

**Function:** Show ideation breadth via a cluster of "How to" questions. Demonstrates divergent thinking before convergent solution.

**Length:** Brief intro sentence + 12+ How-to questions rendered as speech bubbles.

**Structure:**
- **Intro sentence:** "We started ideation by **delving back into the problems of our user**. We renewed our **user personas** with our focus on [CHOSEN PROBLEM AREA] and developed **How-to questions** based on them."

- **12+ How-to questions** rendered as overlapping speech bubbles of varied sizes. Each is 5-15 words. The cluster should span across the chosen problem area's sub-issues.

- **Question construction:** "How to [action verb] [object / outcome] [optional context]?"
  - "How to ensure proper development (physical and cognitive) in today's [CONTEXT]?"
  - "How to provide cultural connections & interactions to [BENEFICIARY]?"
  - "How to bring out [USER] from the deep guilt of [PAINFUL ACTION]?"
  - "How to remove the stigma related to [SOLUTION CATEGORY]?"

- **The questions should fan out across multiple solution-space angles** — not all from one angle. A good cluster has questions about: emotional barriers, practical operations, regulatory/trust, awareness/culture, safety, community-building, healthcare, and stakeholder coordination. The diversity of questions argues "we considered many directions."

- **Visual treatment:** speech bubbles in varied sizes, partially overlapping, in soft coral/cream colors. The cluster should feel like a brainstorm captured on a wall, not a tidy list.

### Page 1, Section 4: Current Scenario → Challenges → Vision

**Function:** A 3-beat horizontal visual compressing the entire project's argument into one row.

**Length:** Three short labels with icons, connected by dashed lines or arrows.

**Structure:**
- **Current Scenario** — what's happening now in the world (1 short phrase, 3-6 words). Example: "Declining female workforce participation."
- **Challenges** — what's preventing change (1 short phrase). Example: "Insufficient Childcare support."
- **Vision** — what the team aims to enable (1 short phrase, action-oriented). Example: "Provide a safe and reliable childcare service."

Each gets a custom icon (typically: a location/people icon for Current Scenario, a person-with-question/obstacle icon for Challenges, a star/aspirational icon for Vision).

This is the **one-line argument structure** of the entire project. If a reader saw only this triple, they should be able to infer the whole project's premise.

### Page 1, Section 5: Co-Creation and Idea Selection

**Function:** Name the dual research methods used to generate and validate the solution + present 3 hero validation statistics.

**Length:** 1-2 sentences of methodology framing + 3 hero stats.

**Structure:**
- **3 hero stats** rendered as large numbers with short captions:
  - **X%** of users approved of our solution
  - **X%** of [USERS] said it's [POSITIVE QUALITY, e.g., trustworthy]
  - **X%** said this would significantly [POSITIVE OUTCOME, e.g., reduce stress]

- **Methodology paragraph:** "We engaged **[N] [USER TYPE] and their [LINKED PARTY] to co-create with us**, which greatly contributed in understanding their needs. Subsequently, we developed **[N] significant ideas** and involved **[LARGER N] [USER TYPE]** using the **A/B testing method** to select the most favorable concept using **a poster featuring storyboards and key service features**."

- **The dual-method claim is critical.** Co-creation (small N, generates the concept) + A/B testing (larger N, validates the concept). Naming both methods with specific n's is what makes the validation stats credible. Without methodology, the stats are floating numbers.

### Page 1, Section 6: Challenges Conquered (sidebar)

**Function:** A research-execution humility move. Names the friction the team encountered *while doing the research itself*, which makes the validation stats above more credible.

**Length:** 3-5 short bullet points in a sidebar.

**Structure:**
- Each bullet is a 6-15 word phrase naming a specific research-execution challenge.
- Examples:
  - "Securing permission to interact with children in a daycare setting"
  - "Involving mothers in the co-creation activities due to their busy schedules"
  - "Executing user testing with unacquainted participants"
  - "Building a rapport with children to gain valuable insights"

- The challenges should be **real research-process challenges**, not project-content challenges. They're about *how hard it was to do the research*, not *how hard the problem is*.

This sidebar is unusual and powerful. Most case studies hide the messiness of research. This one names it. The implicit argument: "The validation data wasn't easy to collect. The fact that we collected it anyway is itself a credibility signal."

### Page 2, Section 1: Concept Description

**Function:** Reveal the named solution with built-in positioning + a tight concept paragraph.

**Length:** Solution name + 1 paragraph (3-5 sentences).

**Structure:**
- **Solution name with built-in meaning:** "**[BrandName] ([Full Form / Tagline])** provides…" The name should *embody* the core mechanic. Examples:
  - "Togit (Together in Time)" — embodies the intergenerational care mechanic
  - "Sanjeevini (Daily Companion)" — could embody an elderly-companion service
  - "Setu (Bridge)" — could embody an inter-generational/inter-community bridging service

- **Concept paragraph** packs in 4 elements:
  1. **Core mechanic** — what fundamentally novel thing the service does
  2. **Economic model** — how it stays affordable / how it sustains itself
  3. **Unit economics** — specific ratios, capacities, or operational constants (e.g., "2-5 child per caretaker ratio")
  4. **Emotional outcome** — what the user feels because of all the above

- **Bolded key phrases** mark the most important concept elements.

Example structure:
> "[Brand] provides an **[adjective] and unique [core mechanic noun phrase]** by **[economic model — how operations stay affordable]**. **[Type of staff]** and **[secondary participant]** offer **[type of attention/care]** with a **[unit-economic ratio]**, **[emotional outcome 1]** while **[emotional outcome 2]**. **[Cost dynamic that maintains affordability]**, **[overall environmental/relational outcome]**."

### Page 2, Section 2: Our Primary Stakeholders

**Function:** Show the design serves more than one population. Visualize the multi-party design.

**Length:** 3-4 named stakeholders with icons + 1 isometric illustration of the service in context.

**Structure:**
- **3-4 stakeholder labels** with custom line-art icons. For the original: Elderly, Mother, Children. These are the participants the service is designed for — not just the user named in the Round 1 HMW.

- **Isometric illustration** showing the service embedded in its environment. For the original: an apartment complex (society) with the Togit center embedded in it. The illustration positions the service spatially — it's not standalone, it's inside a real-world context.

The multi-stakeholder framing implicitly argues: "This solution creates value for multiple parties, not just one." This is what makes the unit economics work (e.g., elderly + children + caretakers all benefit, which is what allows the affordability claim).

### Page 2, Section 3: Key Features (5 features)

**Function:** Detail the 5 key features that make the solution work. Each feature mixes mechanic (how) and emotional outcome (what the user feels).

**Length:** 5 features, each with: custom icon + bolded feature name + 2-4 sentence paragraph.

**Structure of each feature:**
- **Custom icon** depicting the feature's mechanic visually
- **Bolded feature name** — short noun phrase (1-3 words). Examples: "Intergenerational Care," "Transportation," "Space Modularity," "App," "Onboarding Kit."
- **Paragraph** with bolded key phrases inside, following the **mechanic → emotional outcome** pattern:
  - Mechanic sentence(s): how the feature works, who's involved, what the design choice is
  - Emotional outcome sentence(s): what the user feels because of the mechanic

**Feature naming convention:**
- 1-3 words
- Concrete (not abstract)
- Domain-specific (not generic)
- Examples that work: "Intergenerational Care," "Space Modularity," "Onboarding Kit"
- Examples that fail: "User Experience," "Smart Design," "Holistic Approach"

**Feature mix:** the 5 features should span different categories:
1. **The core differentiator** — what makes this solution unique vs. existing alternatives
2. **The convenience play** — how it removes friction users currently have
3. **The cost-control play** — how it stays affordable / sustainable
4. **The digital layer** — the app or technology component
5. **The first-touch experience** — how users onboard / first encounter the service

If all 5 features are variations of the same theme, the design isn't doing enough work.

**For the App feature specifically:** name 3-7 specific app features as bullet points, with at least one feature per major user role (mother, service employees, elderly, etc. depending on the solution).

### Page 3, Section 1: Service Flow

**Function:** A service blueprint showing the user journey + operational flow through illustrated steps.

**Length:** 9-12 numbered steps, each with a custom illustration.

**Structure:**
- **Each step** has:
  - A small custom illustration (figurative — showing people doing the action)
  - A short label below the illustration (2-5 words, action-oriented)

- **Step naming convention:** action-oriented short phrases. Examples:
  - "Set Up"
  - "Spread Awareness"
  - "Enrolment of [User Type]"
  - "Onboarding Kit provided to [User Type]"
  - "Drop off [Subject]"
  - "Induction of [Other User Type]"
  - "Playful activities involving [Multiple parties]"
  - "Regular [Subject] Updates through App"
  - "Meal Time with [Parties]"
  - "Pick Up by [User Type]"
  - "Development of [Systemic Outcome]"

- **Step mix:** the flow should include:
  - **Setup steps** (one-time): Set Up, Spread Awareness, Enrolment
  - **Onboarding steps** (per-user): Onboarding Kit, Induction
  - **Daily operation steps** (recurring): Drop off, Activities, Meal Time, Pick Up
  - **Communication steps** (recurring): Updates through App
  - **Systemic outcome step** (long-term): the emergent benefit over time

- **The final step is special.** It should not be an operational step but an **emergent systemic outcome** — the service's contribution to the broader world. Examples: "Development of an inclusive and happy society," "Building intergenerational community trust." This ends the service flow on a note of macro impact, not operational close.

**Visual treatment:** illustrated steps arranged in a flowing path (sometimes serpentine, sometimes grid) with dashed-line connectors showing sequence.

### Page 3, Section 2: Frequently Asked Questions

**Function:** Anticipate critical questions a skeptical evaluator or user would actually ask. Demonstrate the team has thought through objections.

**Length:** 6-8 FAQs, each with a 2-4 sentence answer.

**Structure of each FAQ:**
- **Q. [Question]?** rendered as a heading
- **Answer paragraph** (2-4 sentences) with bolded key phrases marking the most important assertions

**Question selection — pick from these categories** (aim for at least 5 of these 7 categories represented):

1. **The host community's WIIFM** — Why would the venue/partners benefit? (e.g., "How do societies benefit from this approach?")
2. **The volunteer / staff recruitment question** — How will you get the people involved? (e.g., "How do you plan to incentivise the elderly?")
3. **The edge case** — What happens when something goes wrong operationally? (e.g., "What if I am late to pick up my child?")
4. **The showstopper question** — What is the single most important risk this solution must address, and how? (e.g., "How are you ensuring child safety?")
5. **The inclusivity question** — Does this serve users with special circumstances? (e.g., "What do I do if my child has special needs?")
6. **The staffing/training question** — How do you ensure quality of the people delivering the service? (e.g., "How do you plan to onboard caretakers?")
7. **The operational availability question** — When is this available? Where are the gaps in coverage? (e.g., "Is it available for the entire week or are there any holidays?")

**Answer construction:**
- Each answer must explain **mechanism**, not just policy. "We ensure child safety" is policy. "We provide live CCTV surveillance with facial detection technology to ensure privacy and safety…" is mechanism.
- Each answer should include 2-4 specific, named features or processes from the solution (e.g., "tap-in ID cards," "CCTV with facial detection," "comprehensive background checks").
- Bolded key phrases mark the most important assertions.

**The FAQ section is a critique-anticipation move.** Most case studies skip this. The teams that include substantive FAQs are signaling: "We've stress-tested our concept. We know what you're worried about. Here's how we address it."

### Page 4, Section 1: Benefits of Our Service

**Function:** Summarize the service's benefits via a visual word cloud with varied font sizes.

**Length:** 12-15 short benefit phrases rendered as a word cloud.

**Structure:**
- Each benefit is 2-4 words
- Font size varies by importance / frequency / impact
- Color shades vary across coral/peach tones to add visual rhythm

**Examples:** "Enhanced Trust," "Flexible Service hours," "Familiar Environment," "Reduced Stress," "Easy Pick Up and Drop Off," "Enhance Cultural Connection," "Individual Attention," "Low Cost," "Homely Feeling," "Holistic Child Development," "Enhanced Work Efficiency," "Communal Support," "Increased Security."

**The word cloud format is a deliberate visual rhyme with Round 2's persona painpoints word cloud.** Round 2 mapped pain via word cloud; Round 4 maps benefit via word cloud. The same visual grammar communicates "the pain we identified is now the benefit we deliver."

**Critical:** benefits should map to features. Each benefit should be traceable to a specific feature or mechanic in the solution. A "Reduced Stress" benefit must trace to specific features (location within society, app updates, trusted staff). If a benefit doesn't map to any feature, the team is over-claiming.

### Page 4, Section 2: Types of Well Being Targeted (wellbeing framework return)

**Function:** Return to Round 1's 6-dimension wellbeing radar to demonstrate the solution fulfils Round 1's commitments.

**Length:** A radar chart + a 4-5 sentence paragraph.

**Structure:**
- **The same 6-dimension radar chart** from Round 1 (Physical, Emotional, Environmental, Social, Financial, Occupational — or whatever framework was used in Round 1). The radar shape now visualizes which dimensions the solution actually impacts.

- **Connecting paragraph** with bolded dimension names:
"[Solution] improves [USER]'s **[2 primary dimensions]** through [mechanism]. We foster **[secondary dimension]** for [outcome] across [scope]. Our focus on **[mechanism]** ensures [tertiary beneficiary]'s **[dimension]**, while **[economic/operational choice]** positively impacts the **[environmental/structural dimension]**."

**This is the most important loop-closing move in the brief.** Round 1 stated which wellbeing dimensions the project would target. Round 4 returns to show the solution actually addresses those dimensions. Without this return, the team's Round 1 commitment is unfulfilled. With it, the project comes full circle.

**Check that the dimensions the solution claims to address match (or sensibly expand) the dimensions Round 1 committed to.** If Round 1 said "focus on Emotional and Occupational" and Round 4's solution claims to address all six dimensions equally, something's off — the team is over-claiming.

### Page 4, Section 3: Impact (5 statements)

**Function:** Make the 5-layer impact case grounded in the solution. Each impact should be traceable to a specific feature/mechanic.

**Length:** 5 impact statements, each as a soft-background callout with an icon + headline + 2-3 sentence paragraph.

**Structure of each impact statement:**
- **Custom icon** depicting the beneficiary or outcome
- **Headline** in the format: "[Category]: [Specific framing]"
  - "Promoting [Outcome]: [Mechanism]"
  - "[Action] [Beneficiary] and [supporting outcome]"
  - "[Outcome] and [Outcome]: [Mechanism]"
- **Paragraph** (2-3 sentences) explaining the mechanism and the outcome with bolded key phrases

**The 5 impacts should ripple outward** (same pattern as Round 1 and Round 3):
1. **Primary beneficiary emotional impact** — what improves emotionally for the direct user
2. **Structural / equity impact** — how the solution challenges existing inequities
3. **Community impact** — what builds in the broader community via the solution
4. **Cultural / awareness impact** — what perceptions shift because of the solution
5. **Environmental / sustainability impact** — what improves at the systemic / planetary level

Each impact must be **traceable to a specific feature**. If a claimed impact has no feature backing it, the impact is over-claimed — cut it or add a supporting feature.

### Page 4, Section 4: Future Goals and Scalability

**Function:** Project 3 paths to scale beyond the initial concept.

**Length:** 3 short scalability directions, each with a coral bullet dot and a paragraph with bolded key phrases.

**Structure:**
- **3 distinct growth axes**, NOT 3 variations of the same expansion:
  1. **Supply-chain or resource axis** — managing surplus / waste reduction / circular economy
  2. **Existing-market upgrade axis** — repurposing existing services to enhance quality
  3. **New-venue partnership axis** — partnerships with schools / workplaces / institutions

- Each direction is a 2-3 sentence paragraph with bolded key phrases.

The three axes signal the team has thought about multiple growth paths — not committed prematurely to one. This is the right move at the solution-presentation stage: identify the directions, defer the choice to later.

### Page 4, Section 5: Closing emotional statement

**Function:** Close the brief with a backward-acknowledgment + forward-commitment statement. This is the project's final note.

**Length:** 1 paragraph (3-5 sentences).

**Structure:**

The template:
> "Throughout this challenge, our team developed **[emotional disposition that grew]** for all the [USERS], recognizing **[sacrifices/difficulties the users face]**. Our vision is to **[forward-looking action verb] this journey for these [USERS]** by becoming **[the team's role/identity]** they need to **[outcome the team enables]**, ensuring that **[the core promise]**. **Team [Name] is wholeheartedly dedicated to make this vision a reality.**"

**The two-part structure is essential:**
- **Backward acknowledgment** — what the team felt or learned (empathy for users, recognition of difficulty)
- **Forward commitment** — what the team will do, framed in identity language ("Team [Name] is wholeheartedly dedicated…")

The closing line **names the team again** ("Team Dua is wholeheartedly dedicated to make this vision a reality"). This is the only place in the brief where the team's name appears as a subject doing an action, rather than as a credit line. The naming move converts the team from documenters into agents.

**Team credit line** appears at the bottom of the page, in italic styling, matching all prior rounds.

## Voice and tone

Follow all voice and tone rules from the prior three round skills. First-person plural, hedged claims when appropriate, bolded key phrases, no AI prose tells, empathetic but not saccharine.

**Round 4-specific voice rules:**

- **Confidence and resolution replace exploration.** The team is no longer asking questions — they're presenting. Hedging reduces. Direct claims appear ("Togit provides…," "Each child is paired…").

- **But not arrogance.** The team maintains specificity (named features, named methods, named stats), sourcing (real sources for the hero stat), and mature nuance (Challenges Conquered shows research humility). The voice is *resolved* but not *triumphant*.

- **Feature paragraphs follow the mechanic → emotional outcome pattern.** Each Key Features paragraph starts by explaining how the feature works, then ends with what the user feels because of it. Both halves are required.

- **FAQ answers explain mechanism, not just policy.** "We ensure child safety" is policy. "We provide live CCTV surveillance with facial detection technology…" is mechanism. Always include mechanism.

- **Impact statements name specific causal chains.** "It will help society" is empty. "By removing barriers to women's professional and family aspirations through affordable and accessible childcare, we empower them to excel in their careers…" is causal.

- **The closing statement uses identity language.** "Team [Name] is wholeheartedly dedicated…" — this names the team as the agent of the future commitment. Don't replace this with passive language ("It is our hope that…").

- **No marketing or sales language.** The solution is presented, not pitched. Avoid "revolutionary," "game-changing," "next-generation," "first-of-its-kind." These are marketing words. The solution should sound *credibly designed*, not *aggressively marketed*.

## Visual design language

Follow all visual design rules from prior round skills. Same warm coral / dusty rose / cream palette, custom line-art iconography, wavy section dividers, sans-serif typography.

**Round 4-specific visual elements:**

- **Cause-and-effect tree (Page 1):** A diagram with 5 macro causes (each with custom icon) and sub-branches off the cause-most-connected-to-the-chosen-problem. Lines connecting causes to sub-causes should use a soft dashed style. The hero stat sits near the diagram.

- **Hero stat reprise (Page 1):** Same visual treatment as it had when it first appeared in earlier rounds — large number, brief caption, source cited beneath. Treats the stat as a reactivation, not a new appearance.

- **Need Statement callout (Page 1):** Same visual treatment as in Round 3 — soft-background callout with oversized quotation marks.

- **How-to questions cluster (Page 1):** 12+ speech bubbles in varied sizes, partially overlapping. Use 2-3 shades of coral/peach for visual rhythm. Should feel like a captured brainstorm.

- **Current-Challenges-Vision triple (Page 1):** Three icons in a horizontal row with dashed-line or arrow connectors between them. Each icon labeled with a short phrase below in italic styling.

- **Hero validation stats (Page 1):** Three large numbers in a row (e.g., 74%, 77%, 68%), each with a short caption beneath.

- **Challenges Conquered sidebar (Page 1):** A small offset box with a sidebar-style header and a bulleted list of 3-5 research challenges in italic or muted styling.

- **Stakeholder triangle (Page 2):** 3-4 stakeholder icons arranged in a triangle or row, each labeled in italic.

- **Isometric service illustration (Page 2):** An isometric-perspective illustration of the service embedded in its environmental context (e.g., apartment complex with center in it, or village with service hub embedded). This is the most visually ambitious illustration in the brief.

- **Key features (Page 2):** 5 features in a vertical or two-column layout, each with: custom icon (left), bolded feature name + paragraph (right). For the App feature, a bulleted sub-list of 3-7 app features.

- **Service blueprint (Page 3):** 9-12 illustrated steps arranged in a flowing path. Each step has a small custom illustration (figurative) with a short label in italic beneath. Dashed-line connectors between steps showing sequence.

- **FAQ section (Page 3):** Each Q is rendered in italic, each A in regular text. Generous spacing between FAQs. The questions are visually larger than typical body text to invite scanning.

- **Benefits word cloud (Page 4):** 12-15 benefit phrases in varied font sizes and 2-3 coral/peach color shades. Loose, non-grid arrangement.

- **Wellbeing radar return (Page 4):** Same radar chart from Round 1, with the solution's coverage shape filled in.

- **Impact statements (Page 4):** Each in a soft-background callout box with custom icon, italic headline, and regular-weight paragraph.

- **Scalability dots (Page 4):** Three coral dots with paragraphs beside them. Visual continuity with Round 1's Opportunities Identified treatment.

- **Closing statement (Page 4):** Regular prose styling. The final sentence ("Team [Name] is wholeheartedly dedicated…") is treated visually as more prominent — possibly in coral text or slightly larger weight.

## Source and evidence standards

Follow all source standards from prior skills.

**Round 4-specific sourcing:**

- **Validation statistics must come from real testing.** If the team didn't actually do co-creation with 5 mothers + A/B testing with 70 mothers, the stats are fabricated and the section fails. If the team did smaller-scale testing, report the actual smaller-scale results — don't inflate to match the original brief's numbers.

- **Hero stat reprise must match the earlier round's stat exactly.** Don't re-write the stat or change the citation. Reproduce verbatim.

- **Need Statement reprise must match Round 3's wording exactly.** No edits.

- **App features and service flow steps must reflect real design work.** If the team didn't actually design an app, don't list specific features. Either reduce the App section to "The app will include features for [USER ROLES]" without specifics, or do the design work first.

- **FAQ answers must reflect real planning.** If asked "How are you ensuring child safety?" don't fabricate a CCTV-with-facial-detection plan. Either describe what the team actually planned (even if it's less impressive than the original brief's answer) or flag the FAQ as needing the team's input before draft completion.

- **Scalability directions must be plausible.** Don't invent expansion paths the team hasn't thought about. If the team has only thought about one growth axis, name one — don't fabricate three.

## Common failure modes — read before producing the final output

1. **No backwards-looking opening.** Page 1 starts with the solution or with new information. *Fix:* Page 1 must open with the project recap paragraph in past tense.

2. **Cause-and-effect tree introduces new content.** The 5 macro causes weren't in prior rounds. *Fix:* the tree synthesizes prior research — every cause should be findable in Rounds 1 or 2.

3. **Hero stat is a new statistic.** *Fix:* the Page 1 hero stat should be a reactivation of a stat from earlier rounds, not new data.

4. **Need Statement is paraphrased instead of reproduced verbatim.** *Fix:* reproduce the Round 3 Need Statement word-for-word in a callout.

5. **How-to questions all come from one angle.** All 12 are about logistics or all 12 are about emotional barriers. *Fix:* the cluster should span across multiple angles (emotional, operational, regulatory, cultural, safety, community, healthcare, stakeholder).

6. **Only one research method is named.** Just co-creation, or just A/B testing. *Fix:* the dual-method claim is critical. Co-creation generates the concept; A/B testing validates it. Both must be named with specific n's.

7. **Validation stats with no methodology.** Three percentages floating with no description of how they were gathered. *Fix:* always pair the stats with the dual-method paragraph.

8. **Challenges Conquered sidebar is missing.** *Fix:* add it. It's the research-humility move that makes the validation stats credible.

9. **Solution name has no built-in meaning.** "ChildCare Pro" or "BetterDay." *Fix:* the name should embody the core mechanic. Spend time on naming.

10. **Key features are bullet points without paragraphs.** *Fix:* each feature needs a 2-4 sentence paragraph following the mechanic → emotional outcome pattern. Bullets only appear inside the App feature for sub-features.

11. **Service flow ends on an operational step.** "Pick Up by Mother" is the last step. *Fix:* end on an emergent systemic outcome step ("Development of an inclusive and happy society" or analogous).

12. **FAQs are softball questions.** "What is Togit?" "How does Togit work?" *Fix:* FAQs must be substantive critical questions a skeptical evaluator would ask. Pull from the 7 categories in the FAQ playbook.

13. **FAQ answers are policy, not mechanism.** "We ensure safety." *Fix:* explain *how* — name specific features, processes, or technologies.

14. **Benefits word cloud has benefits that don't map to features.** *Fix:* each benefit should trace back to a specific feature or mechanic. Cut benefits that don't.

15. **No return to the wellbeing framework from Round 1.** Page 4 omits the radar or replaces it with a different framework. *Fix:* return to the exact framework Round 1 used. This is the most important loop-closing move.

16. **Wellbeing claims expand beyond Round 1's commitment.** Round 1 said "focus on Emotional + Occupational" but Round 4 claims the solution addresses all 6 dimensions equally. *Fix:* be honest about which dimensions the solution actually targets. Modest claims preserve credibility.

17. **Impact statements without feature traceability.** "It will help society be more equitable." *Fix:* every impact must be traceable to a specific feature or mechanic. If a claimed impact has no feature backing it, cut or add a supporting feature.

18. **Scalability directions are 3 variations of the same axis.** All three are about expanding venue partnerships. *Fix:* the three should be different growth axes (resource/supply chain, existing-market upgrade, new-venue partnership).

19. **Closing statement is corporate/empty.** "We are proud of what we've accomplished." *Fix:* the closing needs both backward acknowledgment (what the team felt/learned) AND forward commitment (what the team will do, in identity language with team name).

20. **Marketing language slips in.** "Revolutionary," "next-generation," "game-changing." *Fix:* the brief presents, doesn't pitch. Cut marketing words.

## Worked mini-example (adapted to a new problem statement)

Continuing the elderly-loneliness example from the prior skills. **Illustrative only.**

**Carrying over from prior rounds:**
- Round 1 HMW: "How might we help elderly individuals living alone in urban India sustain meaningful daily connection?"
- Round 3 Need Statement: "Urban Indian elderly living alone need a dignified, culturally-rooted form of daily companionship and check-in so that they can feel anchored to a community and age with autonomy rather than abandonment."
- Round 1 wellbeing focus: Emotional + Social
- Round 2-3 hero stat (illustrative): "41% of urban Indian elderly report experiencing chronic loneliness. Source: NSSO survey."

**Round 4 Page 1 — Background + Cause and Effect + Ideation + Validation:**

Background: "Throughout the course of this challenge, our objective was to thoroughly examine the various obstacles that urban Indian elderly living alone encounter in sustaining meaningful daily connection, and the impact it has on their overall well-being."

Cause and Effect Tree:
- 5 macro causes: Adult children migrating for work, Erosion of joint family norms, Loss of work-based identity post-retirement, Mobility decline, **Absence of dignified peer-companionship infrastructure** (this branches further)
- Sub-causes off the 5th: Stigma of attending senior centres, Cultural mismatch in existing programs, Limited operating hours, Transport gap, Underfunded NGOs, Cultural-language mismatch with younger volunteers

Hero stat reprise: 41% chronic loneliness stat.

Need Statement reprise: full Round 3 statement.

How-to questions (15+): How to design senior connection without stigma? How to bridge intergenerational gap respectfully? How to make daily companionship affordable? How to involve neighbors meaningfully? How to integrate cultural and spiritual practice into companionship? How to handle health emergencies as a companion service? How to scale across language regions? Etc.

Current → Challenges → Vision: "Rising urban elderly population living alone" → "Stigma + infrastructure gap in formal services" → "Provide dignified culturally-rooted daily companionship."

Co-Creation + A/B testing methodology: "We engaged 6 elderly individuals and their adult children to co-create with us. Subsequently, we developed three significant ideas and involved 50 elderly + adult-children pairs using A/B testing to select the most favorable concept."

Validation stats: "82% approved of our solution. 76% of elderly users said it preserves their dignity. 69% of adult children said it would reduce their long-distance caregiving anxiety."

Challenges Conquered: Building trust with elderly participants who were hesitant to discuss loneliness. Coordinating co-creation across multiple language preferences. Executing A/B testing remotely with low-tech-fluency participants. Etc.

**Round 4 Page 2 — Concept Description + Stakeholders + Key Features:**

Concept: "Sanjeevini (Daily Companion) provides a dignified, culturally-rooted daily companionship and check-in service by partnering with college students seeking cultural mentorship, retired professionals seeking purpose, and local cultural organizations. Trained matchmakers pair elderly individuals with 1-2 weekly companions for in-person visits supplemented by daily voice-only phone check-ins, fostering connection without forcing technology adoption. The peer-to-peer + intergenerational hybrid model addresses both companionship and identity-purpose needs while keeping the service affordable."

Primary Stakeholders: Elderly Individual, Companion (student/retired professional), Adult Child (remote sponsor), Cultural Community Partner.

5 Key Features:
1. **Cultural Matchmaking** — pairs based on language, religious practice, food preferences, vocational background
2. **In-Person + Voice-Only Hybrid** — weekly in-person visits + daily voice check-ins (no video required, respecting elderly tech preferences)
3. **Family Sponsorship Model** — adult children abroad sponsor the service for their parents, paying in foreign currency that funds local companions
4. **Companion App** — for companions and family sponsors (elderly use a simple voice-only phone line)
5. **Onboarding Ritual** — first meeting includes a shared meal at the elderly person's home, framed culturally to reduce sense of "receiving service"

**Round 4 Page 3 — Service Flow + FAQs:**

Service Flow (10 steps): Set Up partnerships → Recruit companions → Outreach to elderly + families → Cultural matching → Onboarding ritual → Weekly in-person visit → Daily voice check-in → Family sponsor updates via app → Quarterly community gathering → Building dignified ageing-in-community.

FAQs (7):
- How does this preserve elderly dignity? (the showstopper)
- How do you incentivise college students to participate?
- What if my parent doesn't want a stranger visiting? (the edge case)
- How do you handle health emergencies during visits?
- What if my parent speaks a regional language?
- How does the family-sponsor model work financially?
- What if my parent prefers solitude on some days?

**Round 4 Page 4 — Benefits + Wellbeing Return + Impact + Scalability + Closing:**

Benefits word cloud: Cultural Continuity, Reduced Loneliness, Preserved Dignity, Family Peace of Mind, Intergenerational Learning, etc.

Wellbeing return: Same radar from Round 1. Solution coverage on Emotional + Social (primary), with secondary coverage on Cultural Wellbeing (Round 1 framework note: this might need expansion if the original framework was 6-dim wellbeing).

Impact (5): Elderly emotional wellbeing; Intergenerational understanding; Community fabric of urban India; Cultural transmission; Economic sustainability of dignified ageing.

Scalability: Cross-language regional expansion; Partnership with religious/cultural organizations; Integration with healthcare check-ins.

Closing: "Throughout this challenge, our team developed an intense empathy for all the elderly individuals who live alone, recognizing the dignity they preserve even in isolation. Our vision is to ease this journey by becoming the cultural bridge they need to age with autonomy and community, ensuring that no elderly individual has to choose between independence and loneliness. **Team [Name] is wholeheartedly dedicated to make this vision a reality.**"

## Scoring Benchmark

Before delivering any Round 4 brief, score it on the four dimensions below.

| Dimension | What It Measures | Target |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| **AI-Likeness** | Generic AI prose, marketing language, empty closing, fabricated stats, missing methodology. Voice should match prior rounds with the slight Round 4 shift toward confidence-and-resolution without losing empathy or specificity. | 1–3 |
| **Research-to-Solution Traceability** | Does Page 1 open with the project recap? Does the cause-and-effect tree synthesize prior rounds without introducing new content? Is the hero stat reactivated (not new)? Is the Need Statement reproduced verbatim from Round 3? Do features and impacts trace back to specific needs/pain points from prior rounds? Does Page 4 return to Round 1's wellbeing framework? | 8–10 |
| **Solution Concreteness** | Is the solution named with built-in meaning? Are 5 key features specified with mechanics + emotional outcomes (not bullets)? Are 9-12 service flow steps illustrated and named action-orientedly? Does the flow end on an emergent systemic outcome? Are 6-8 FAQs substantive (not softball) with mechanism-explaining answers? | 8–10 |
| **Validation & Impact Sharpness** | Are dual research methods named (co-creation + A/B testing) with specific n's? Are validation stats real (not fabricated)? Is the Challenges Conquered sidebar present? Do the 5 impact statements ripple outward AND trace to specific features? Are the 3 scalability directions at distinguishable axes? Does the closing combine backward acknowledgment + forward commitment with identity language? | 8–10 |

### What each dimension looks like at low vs. high scores

**AI-Likeness (lower is better)**
- *1–3 (target):* Reads like the same team that wrote prior rounds. Confident but not triumphant. Specific named features, methods, stats. Empathy preserved through closing statement.
- *7–10 (fail):* Marketing language slips in. Closing is generic ("we hope to make a difference"). Features described in vague positive adjectives without mechanics.

**Research-to-Solution Traceability (higher is better)**
- *8–10 (target):* Project recap opens Page 1. Cause-and-effect tree synthesizes Rounds 1-3 cleanly. Hero stat is a reactivation. Need Statement reproduced verbatim. Wellbeing radar returns. Each feature/impact maps to research from prior rounds.
- *1–4 (fail):* Page 1 introduces new content. Cause-and-effect tree has causes that don't appear in earlier rounds. Stats are new. Need Statement paraphrased or absent. No wellbeing return.

**Solution Concreteness (higher is better)**
- *8–10 (target):* Named solution with built-in positioning. 5 features with mechanic + outcome paragraphs. 9-12 illustrated service-flow steps ending on emergent outcome. 6+ substantive FAQs with mechanism-explaining answers.
- *1–4 (fail):* Unnamed or generically named solution. Features as bullet points without mechanics. Service flow with 3-4 generic steps. Softball FAQs.

**Validation & Impact Sharpness (higher is better)**
- *8–10 (target):* Dual research methods with specific n's. Real validation stats. Challenges Conquered present. Impact statements ripple AND trace to features. 3 distinct scalability axes. Closing uses identity language ("Team X is dedicated…").
- *1–4 (fail):* Single method or no method named. Fabricated stats. No research-humility sidebar. Impact platitudes. Scalability variations of one axis. Generic closing.

### Scoring output format

```
## Self-Audit Scores

| Dimension | Score | Justification |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| AI-Likeness | X/10 | [one sentence] |
| Research-to-Solution Traceability | X/10 | [one sentence] |
| Solution Concreteness | X/10 | [one sentence] |
| Validation & Impact Sharpness | X/10 | [one sentence] |
```

### Important diagnostic cross-checks

- **Solution Concreteness high AND Research-to-Solution Traceability low** → The team designed a polished solution but didn't connect it back to their research. The reader sees a beautiful concept but can't tell why this concept emerged from these specific findings. Re-link features to prior pain points.
- **Validation & Impact Sharpness high AND Solution Concreteness low** → The team validated something but the something is vague. The validation data sits on a thin design foundation. Tighten the feature descriptions and service flow.
- **Research-to-Solution Traceability high AND Solution Concreteness low** → The team is over-explaining the connection back and under-specifying the solution itself. Reduce loop-closure language, increase mechanical detail.
- **All dimensions high but the wellbeing-framework return is missing** → A specific failure mode worth catching independently. Page 4 must return to Round 1's framework.

If any dimension scores below target, revise before delivering.

## Operating procedure

When the user invokes this skill:

1. **Verify Rounds 1, 2, and 3 exist.** Confirm all three prior briefs are available. If any is missing, suggest the user complete it before Round 4.

2. **Identify the loop-closure anchors.** Note: the Round 1 wellbeing framework, the Round 3 Need Statement (verbatim), the most powerful hero stat from earlier rounds, and the chosen problem area from Round 3. These will appear in Round 4 unchanged.

3. **Verify inputs against the input checklist.** Read `references/input-checklist.md`. If mandatory inputs are missing, ask before drafting.

4. **Verify the solution has been actually designed and tested.** Round 4 cannot fabricate the solution. If the team hasn't done co-creation and A/B testing, the validation stats will be fake. Ask honestly: did the team do the design work? If not, the brief either needs to be drafted with smaller-scale honest numbers, or the team needs to do the design work first.

5. **Do real research** for any new external data needed (e.g., scaling-related market data).

6. **Plan the loop closure.** Before drafting, walk through the 10-move arc and verify each closure-point connects to a specific prior-round element: recap, cause-tree synthesis, hero-stat reprise, need-statement reprise, wellbeing return, etc.

7. **Draft section by section** following the playbook. For each section, output prose + statistics + "Visual elements to design" callout.

8. **Audit against the 20 failure modes.**

9. **Self-score using the Scoring Benchmark.** Revise any dimension below target before delivery. Include the score table in the output.

10. **Deliver as a single document** structured across 4 pages with the score table at the end.

11. **Offer next steps.** Ask whether the user wants:
   - A visual HTML/SVG mockup of any specific section (the cause-and-effect tree, the isometric service illustration, the service blueprint, and the FAQ section all benefit from mockups)
   - A second iteration on a dimension where the score missed target
   - The complete case study packaged as a portfolio piece (combining all 4 rounds with consistent design)
