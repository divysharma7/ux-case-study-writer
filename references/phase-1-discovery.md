# Discovery-Brief Case Study (Team Dua style)

This skill produces a 4-page research-and-definition brief in the visual and narrative style of the "Team Dua" working-mothers wellbeing case study. It is the **Discovery + Definition** phase of a service-design project — the deliverable that takes the reader from "here is a broad social context" to "here is a sharply scoped problem worth solving." It does NOT cover solution design, ideation, prototyping, or testing — those are later rounds.

The output is a **content + layout specification**. Claude writes the prose, statistics callouts, insight blocks, journey map, problem statement, and opportunity areas, and specifies the visual elements needed (charts, icons, dividers, layout). The user takes that spec into Figma, Canva, InDesign, or similar to produce the final designed PDF.

---

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the user asks for:

- A portfolio case study in a UX / service-design / design-research style
- A "Round 1," "Phase 1," "Discovery," or "Define" deliverable
- A research brief that synthesizes desk research + user interviews into a problem statement
- A case study "like Team Dua's" or "like this one" (with a similar attached brief as reference)
- A How-Might-We problem statement with supporting research and opportunity areas
- An emotional arc / user journey map as part of a larger research write-up

If the user asks for the *solution* phase (ideation, wireframes, service blueprint, prototypes), this skill is not the right one — but the user may still want it as a *precursor* deliverable, so you can offer it.

---

## The core mental model: the Funnel

The single most important pattern in this style is **progressive narrowing**. Every section is more specific than the one before it. The reader is gently guided from "a broad world I sort of know about" to "a specific person in a specific moment whose problem I now care about."

The funnel runs:

1. **Global context** — the issue exists at a worldwide scale
2. **National / regional context** — it has a specific shape in this geography
3. **Demographic context** — within that geography, one subgroup carries the weight
4. **Individual voices** — what those people actually say and feel
5. **Journey** — what happens to them over time
6. **Synthesis** — which dimensions of life are affected, what opportunities exist
7. **Problem statement** — the precise question the rest of the project will answer

If your draft does not narrow at each step, the funnel is broken. Re-check it before producing the final output.

---

## Document anatomy (4 pages)

| Page | Sections | Job to be done |
|------|----------|----------------|
| 1 | Introduction + Global Scenario | Establish why this topic matters globally; show breadth of problem |
| 2 | National/Regional Perspective + Impact on [Sub-group] + Interview Insights | Narrow to geography + demographic; introduce human voices |
| 3 | Emotional Arc | Plot the affected person's emotional journey over time |
| 4 | Types of Wellbeing Affected + Opportunities Identified + Problem Statement & Expected Impact | Synthesize; scope; pose the HMW question; promise impact |

Section names can be adapted to the problem at hand (e.g., "The Indian Perspective" might become "The Urban Perspective" or "The Gig Economy Perspective"), but the **functional role** of each section must remain.

## Section-by-section writing playbook

### Page 1, Section 1: Introduction

**Function:** Tell the origin story of the team's research. Not "here is the topic," but "here is how we arrived at the topic."

**Length:** 60–100 words across two short paragraphs.

**Structure:**
- Paragraph 1 (3–4 sentences): Methodology-as-narrative. Use the verb chain "we initiated… we conducted… through this process we discovered…" Name the broad starting field (e.g., wellbeing, productivity, urban mobility) and the specific narrowing (e.g., women's wellbeing → working mothers).
- Paragraph 2 (1 sentence): A clean **objective statement**. "Our objective is to design a [service / system / intervention] that addresses [specific need] of [specific user] and improves their [specific outcome]."

**Voice:** First-person plural ("we," "our"). Active, methodological, slightly humble.

**Template:**
```
We initiated our research by delving into the concept of [BROAD FIELD] and
the different perspectives and meanings it holds for different individuals
and groups. We conducted [METHOD 1], [METHOD 2], and [METHOD 3] to gain a
deeper understanding of the topic. Through this process, we were able to
discover that [SPECIFIC SUB-PROBLEM], particularly with respect to
[KEY TENSION], is vastly under-represented and overlooked in current scenario.

Our objective is to design a [DELIVERABLE TYPE] that addresses the specific
needs of [TARGET USER] in terms of their [SPECIFIC ASPECT], and improves
their overall [OUTCOME].
```

### Page 1, Section 2: The Global Scenario

**Function:** Establish that this is a worldwide pattern. Pair acknowledgment of progress with the persistent problem.

**Length:** 60–90 words of prose + 1 categorized problem cluster + 2 hero statistics.

**Structure — the 4-beat rhythm:**
1. **Acknowledge progress** ("The global scenario for [TOPIC] has seen major progress in recent years, through [GAINS]…")
2. **Pivot to the problem** ("However, they still face [BIAS / GAP / FRICTION]…")
3. **Anchor in recency** — name a contemporary event or trend (pandemic, AI shift, economic event, climate event) that has changed or worsened the picture.
4. **Visual evidence** — the problem cluster + 2 stats.

**The problem cluster** is a categorized visual list. NOT bullet points. Two named buckets, with 4–6 short labels each. The bucket names already telegraph the eventual focus of the case study — choose them deliberately.

Example bucket-naming pattern:
- Working women → "Personal Occupation" / "Family Responsibilities"
- Mental health at work → "Workplace Factors" / "Personal Factors"
- Student loneliness → "Academic Pressures" / "Social Barriers"

**The two hero statistics** must form a complementary pair:
- **Pair type A — disparity + severity:** one stat shows a gap between groups (X% vs Y%), the other shows how severe the impact is (Z% experience the worst outcome).
- **Pair type B — prevalence + consequence:** one stat shows how common the issue is, the other shows what it costs.

Each statistic must have:
- A large numeric callout (e.g., **43%**)
- A short sentence explaining what the number means
- A named source ("Source: [Publication or Study]")

### Page 2, Section 1: The [National / Regional] Perspective

**Function:** Narrow from global to a specific geography or context. Set up the comparative framing that justifies the rest of the case study.

**Length:** 50–80 words + 2 statistics.

**Structure:**
- **Comparative opener:** "While [BETTER-OFF CONTEXT] has various systems for [WHAT], [YOUR CONTEXT] is still struggling." This contrast must be honest, not strawmanned — name a real gap.
- **Barriers paragraph:** "There exist numerous barriers which lead to [OUTCOME]."
- **Belief stat + behavior stat:** A two-stat pair where the first shows *what people think* (a cultural/belief number) and the second shows *what's actually happening* (a behavior/outcome number). The structure implicitly argues that belief drives behavior.

Belief stat format: "7 in 10 people [BELIEVE X]." Use icons or a small repeating-figure visual.
Behavior stat format: "[%] [GROUP] [DID/EXPERIENCED X] in [TIMEFRAME]." Use a single large percentage with a brief sentence.

### Page 2, Section 2: Impact on [Sub-group]

**Function:** Further narrow from the national context to the specific sub-population this case study will focus on. Make the **causal claim** that justifies that focus.

**Length:** 50–80 words + 2 statistics on an evidence ladder.

**Structure:**
- **Causal claim:** "While there are many reasons for [GROUP TO DO X], [SPECIFIC CAUSE] has often been recognised as a primary cause."
- **Constraining qualifier:** "Studies show that the majority of [GROUP CITING THIS CAUSE] are [SUB-SUB-GROUP]." This is the move that narrows the focus from "everyone" to "this specific moment in this specific life."
- **The evidence ladder of stats** — two visualizations that progressively constrain the population:
  - First: a comparison showing the sub-group is disproportionately affected (e.g., a progress bar comparing "all working women" to "mothers with children <6 yrs old: 30%")
  - Second: a within-group statistic showing how dominant the cause is for that sub-group (e.g., a donut showing "80% of new mothers not working cited child responsibility as the reason")

### Page 2, Section 3: Interview Insights

**Function:** Introduce human voices. Show the research is grounded in real people, not just secondary stats.

**Length:** ~150 words across opener + 3 insight blocks + 1 pull quote + 2 closing nuance paragraphs.

**Structure:**

1. **Methodology-as-passing-mention opener (1 sentence):** "We interviewed [LIST OF PERSPECTIVES — at least 3, ideally 4]." The list should triangulate: the affected person + a person who chose differently + a domain expert + a related stakeholder (e.g., family member, employer, etc.). This sentence does the work of an entire methodology section without ever being labeled "Methodology."

2. **Three insight blocks**, each with the same structure:
   - **Custom icon** (specify what the icon should depict)
   - **Bolded key phrase** within the prose (NOT the whole sentence — just the 2–4 word phrase that names the insight: "time management," "primarily the woman's responsibility," "stress and guilt")
   - **2 sentences** of supporting context

   The three insights should cover three distinct *flavors* of struggle: an operational/practical one, a relational/social one, and an emotional/internal one. This trio coverage is important — it prevents the research from feeling one-dimensional.

3. **A pull quote** (1–2 sentences) with attribution. The attribution should ideally name someone with **dual standing** — they are both an expert AND a member of the affected group (e.g., "A Psychologist & A Mother"). This dual identity gives the quote unusual authority. Format with oversized quotation marks.

4. **Two closing nuance paragraphs** — this is the most overlooked but most important move:
   - First paragraph: acknowledge a related stakeholder's perspective (e.g., children, partners, employers) and what they understand or feel.
   - Second paragraph: name the **resilience or meaning** the affected group still finds despite the difficulties. "Despite these difficulties, many [GROUP] still find a sense of [PURPOSE / MEANING / SATISFACTION] in [WHAT THEY DO]."

   Without this nuance, the case study reads as a pity-fest. With it, the case study reads as mature ethnographic observation.

### Page 3: Emotional Arc

**Function:** A full-page visual artifact plotting the affected person's emotional journey over time. This is the most distinctive page in the entire brief.

**Structure:**
- **Top caption (1–2 sentences):** "The emotional arc depicts the various stages of a [USER]'s life from [START] till [END]. It encompasses the various emotions and thought processes [THEY] go through as a [ROLE WITH NEW RESPONSIBILITIES]."
- **Scope disclaimer (1 sentence, bolded or highlighted):** "Here we focus on [SPECIFIC TRAJECTORY — e.g., new mothers who leave work to take care of their child]." This sentence is critically important: it admits the arc is one trajectory, not the universal one. It is a research integrity move.

- **5 stages as columns**, in chronological order. The stages should be life-phase labels (not feelings). Examples:
  - For working mothers: Pregnancy → Maternity Leave → Return to the Job → Finding Child Care → Career Challenges
  - For a different problem you'd derive 5 analogous stages.

- **An emotion curve** (Y-axis = emotion, X-axis = time) running across all 5 columns. The curve should:
  - Have 8–12 plotted touchpoints across the 5 stages
  - Zigzag — never a smooth monotonic decline (that's melodrama). Real journeys have local highs and lows.
  - End at the trajectory's natural endpoint (e.g., "Eventually leaves job," "Finally gets help," "Finds a sustainable rhythm")

- **Each touchpoint** has:
  - A short label (3–6 words): "Gets pregnant," "No proper policies on maternal care," "Gives quitting job a thought"
  - An emoji indicator at the curve point (😊 / 😐 / 😞) — repeating the emotional state in visual form
  - Connecting line to the label

- **Two or three small icons** scattered across the arc, anchored to specific stages, depicting the visual essence of that stage (e.g., a briefcase icon at "Return to the Job," a parent-child icon at "Maternity Leave").

**The trajectory shape matters.** The original arc descends in a zigzag — emotional highs followed by deeper lows. This shape implicitly argues that the system fails the user *gradually*, through accumulated friction, not a single event. Choose your arc's shape to match your argument:
- **Descending zigzag** — system fails through accumulated friction (Team Dua's choice)
- **Ascending zigzag** — user finds their way despite obstacles
- **U-shape** — things get worse before they get better (a turning-point story)
- **Flat with sharp drop** — a single shock event reshapes everything

### Page 4, Section 1: Types of Wellbeing Affected (or equivalent framework section)

**Function:** Reference a structured framework that lets you scope which dimensions of the problem you're addressing. Avoid solutionism by being explicit about what you are NOT trying to fix.

**Length:** 40–70 words + 1 framework visualization.

**Structure:**
- **Framework visualization:** A 6-point radar/spider chart with the 6 wellbeing dimensions: Physical, Emotional, Environmental, Social, Financial, Occupational. (For a non-wellbeing problem, substitute an appropriate framework — e.g., needs hierarchy, capability dimensions, service ecosystem layers — but keep it to 5–7 named dimensions visualized as a single integrated diagram.)
- **Scoping prose:** "The relationship between [DIMENSIONS] is interconnected, however, our focus is primarily on promoting [2 NAMED DIMENSIONS]. By addressing these areas, we anticipate a positive ripple effect on [OTHER DIMENSIONS]."

The phrase "we anticipate a positive ripple effect" is key — it admits humility (you can't fix everything) while still claiming reasonable scope.

### Page 4, Section 2: Opportunities Identified

**Function:** Name 3 directional opportunity areas — NOT solutions. These are spaces where intervention could happen, preserving design freedom for Round 2.

**Length:** 30–50 words of prose + 3 short opportunity labels in a branching diagram.

**Structure:**
- **The key insight prefix:** "Based on our research, we have determined that [2 KEY QUALITIES — e.g., 'trust and flexibility'] are crucial elements for [USER] that are currently lacking…"
- **3 opportunity labels**, each 4–7 words, branching from a central node. Each opportunity is a **direction**, not a solution:
  - ✓ "Modifying existing work policies" (direction)
  - ✗ "Building a mobile app for childcare scheduling" (solution — too specific for this stage)

The three opportunities should span different **systemic layers**:
- One that targets policies/structures
- One that targets community/social fabric
- One that targets infrastructure/facilities/tools

This trio coverage prevents the solution space from being lopsided.

### Page 4, Section 3: Problem Statement & Expected Impact

**Function:** The final, sharp How-Might-We question that the rest of the project will answer. Plus a promise of impact that ranges from individual to systemic.

**Length:** 1 HMW sentence + 60–90 words of context + 4 short impact labels.

**The HMW formula** has three precise parts:
1. **Soft verb:** "help" — not "solve," not "fix," not "eliminate." This signals partnership, not paternalism.
2. **Sharply scoped user:** Not "working mothers" — "working women who are in early stages of motherhood." Notice the specificity: a *phase of life*, not just a role.
3. **Aspirational outcome:** "attain a sustainable work-life balance." The word "sustainable" is doing a lot of work — it implies "not just survive, but maintain over time."

**Formula:**
```
How might we help [SHARPLY SCOPED USER IN A SPECIFIC LIFE PHASE]
[SOFT VERB OF ACHIEVING] [ASPIRATIONAL OUTCOME WITH AN ENDURANCE WORD]?
```

Format the HMW question in a **callout/quote bubble with oversized quotation marks** — visually treat it like the most important sentence in the document, because it is.

**Below the HMW**, a **structural context paragraph** explaining why-now: name a societal or structural shift that makes this problem worse or more urgent today than before (e.g., "As nuclear families continue to increase in prevalence…"). Close with a sentence calling for "comprehensive care systems" or analogous broad-frame language that justifies the breadth of solution-space you'll explore.

**4 expected impacts**, each 6–12 words, branching from the HMW. Phrase each as a positive future outcome. The 4 impacts should expand in scope:

1. **Individual** — affects the person directly (e.g., "reduced stress and improved mental and physical health")
2. **Immediate relational** — affects someone closely tied to the user (e.g., "positive impact on development and wellbeing of the child")
3. **Family/work** — affects the user's broader life systems (e.g., "better familial relationship and increased efficiency at work")
4. **Societal** — affects the world at large (e.g., "making it easier for more women to join/rejoin the workforce")

If your 4 impacts are all at the individual level, you're under-claiming. If all at the societal level, you're over-claiming. The expanding ripple is the move.

**Team credit:** At the bottom, in italics or a softer style, the line: "Team [NAME]: [Member 1], [Member 2], [Member 3], [Member 4]."

## Voice and tone rules

**Pronoun:** First-person plural throughout. "We," "our," "we anticipate." Never "I." Never "the researchers."

**Tense:** Past tense for research actions ("We interviewed…"), present tense for findings ("Working mothers face…"), future-conditional for impacts ("we anticipate…").

**Hedging:** Soft hedge most claims. "Often face," "many of which," "may have," "anticipate." Avoid absolute claims unless backed by a cited statistic.

**Bolding:** Bold only **key phrases** (2–4 words), never whole sentences. The bolded text should be a meaningful **noun phrase** that names the concept (e.g., **family responsibilities**, **time management**, **trust and flexibility**) — never a verb or a connector.

**Statistics in prose:** Every numeric claim must have a named source. If a source isn't available, either find one or remove the claim — don't fabricate.

**Empathetic but not saccharine:** Show the difficulty, but always include a counterweight. Phrases like "Despite these difficulties…" and "find a sense of purpose…" are signature moves.

**Avoid:**
- Corporate jargon ("synergize," "leverage," "ecosystem of solutions")
- Solutionism in the research phase ("we will build an app that…")
- Melodrama ("women are crushed by an impossible burden")
- Heavy-handed feminist or political framing — the case study lets data and quotes do the persuading
- Generic AI-prose tells: "delve into," "navigate the complexities of," "in today's fast-paced world"
- "Comprehensive," "holistic," "robust" as filler adjectives (use them only when they actually mean something)

## Visual design language

The user will design the final document themselves, but Claude should specify the visual elements explicitly in the output. Provide a "Visual elements to design" callout for each section.

**Palette:** Soft pastels. Primary: a warm coral / dusty rose (#E89598-ish). Secondary: a cream/peach background (#FCF4ED-ish). Accent: a deeper rose for emphasis. Text: a dark plum or warm brown rather than black.

**Typography:** Sans-serif throughout. Display weight for section headers, regular for body. Statistics rendered LARGE (3–5x body size).

**Iconography:** Custom line-art icons. Single weight, rounded corners. Subjects are figurative (people, objects) not abstract. Each insight gets a unique icon — never reuse.

**Dividers:** Wavy horizontal lines between major sections within a page. Soft, hand-drawn feel.

**Layout:** 2-column predominantly. Whitespace generous. Each page feels uncrowded.

**Charts and visualizations needed:**
- Page 1: A categorized problem cluster (two named buckets, 4–6 labels each). Two statistic callouts with large numbers and sources.
- Page 2: A repeating-figure icon group ("7 in 10 people"). A large circle/donut statistic. A progress bar comparison. A donut chart with two overlaid percentages. Three insight icons. A quote callout with oversized quotation marks.
- Page 3: A 5-column journey map with an emotion curve, 8–12 touchpoints with emoji indicators, and 2–3 anchor icons.
- Page 4: A 6-point radar chart. A branching diagram for opportunities. A callout/quote bubble for the HMW. A branching diagram for expected impacts.

**Quote callouts:** Use oversized quotation marks (e.g., 99-style glyph) at the start. Attribution in italics below.

## Source and evidence standards

The original Team Dua brief cites sources like "Business Insider," "FSG GLOW," "Indeed," "My Perfect Resume." Be honest about source quality:

- **Preferred sources:** Government statistics, peer-reviewed studies, established research organizations (Pew, McKinsey, BCG, FSG, university research centers), reputable journalism (Reuters, FT, Economist, NYT, Indian Express, The Hindu, BBC).
- **Acceptable sources:** Industry reports (LinkedIn, Indeed, etc.), advocacy organizations with strong methodology.
- **Use sparingly:** Listicle sites, brand blogs, surveys with unclear methodology.
- **Never:** Made-up statistics. If a statistic can't be sourced, it must be removed or replaced with a sourced one.

If running this skill for a new problem statement, do a real web search for current statistics and cite them properly. Search for:
- 1–2 global statistics for Page 1
- 2 national/regional statistics for Page 2 (one belief, one behavior)
- 2 sub-group statistics for Page 2 (one comparative, one within-group)
- Any additional supporting numbers for Page 4 if relevant

## Common failure modes — read before producing the final output

1. **The funnel didn't actually narrow.** Each section is roughly the same scope as the last. *Fix:* check that each section's noun phrase is more specific than the previous (e.g., "working women" → "working mothers in India" → "new mothers with children under 6" → "Shikha Handa's experience").

2. **Statistics aren't paired.** A section has one stat, or three, or two unrelated ones. *Fix:* enforce the one-two punch — every section needs exactly two complementary stats, and you should be able to name how they complement (disparity + severity, belief + behavior, prevalence + cause).

3. **Methodology has its own section.** *Fix:* delete it. Weave methodology into the Introduction's "we conducted…" sentence and the Interview Insights opener.

4. **Insights are flat / generic.** "Working mothers face many challenges." *Fix:* each insight needs a bolded key phrase that names a specific concept ("time management," "primarily the woman's responsibility").

5. **No nuance / no resilience paragraph.** The Interview Insights section ends on tragedy. *Fix:* add the 2 closing nuance paragraphs — related stakeholder's understanding + the affected group's source of meaning.

6. **The emotional arc is monotonic.** It just slopes down (or up). *Fix:* introduce local highs and lows — a zigzag. Real life is not monotonic.

7. **No scope disclaimer on the arc.** The arc is presented as universal. *Fix:* add the explicit "Here we focus on…" sentence.

8. **Opportunities are solutions.** "Build an app." *Fix:* convert each into a direction — "Improve [SYSTEM]," "Provide [SUPPORT] within [CONTEXT]."

9. **HMW question is too broad or too narrow.** Too broad: "How might we help women?" Too narrow: "How might we help working mothers in Mumbai aged 28–34 with children under 2 find part-time consulting work?" *Fix:* the user should be a *life phase*, not a role. "Working women who are in early stages of motherhood" — phase, scoped but generative.

10. **Expected impacts don't ripple outward.** All 4 are about the individual, or all 4 are about society. *Fix:* one individual, one immediate-relational, one family/work-system, one societal.

11. **AI-prose tells.** "Navigate the complexities," "delve into," "in today's fast-paced world," "robust solutions," "holistic approach." *Fix:* rewrite in plainer, more specific language. If you can't make a sentence work without these crutches, the sentence isn't saying anything.

12. **Solution leakage in the research phase.** Sentences like "Our app will…" or "The proposed platform…" *Fix:* this is Round 1. No solutions yet. Even opportunity areas are directions, not products.

## Worked mini-example (adapted to a new problem statement)

To show the pattern's adaptability, here is a sketch of how this skill applies to a different topic. **This is illustrative only** — do not output this when the user asks for a real brief.

**Topic:** Loneliness among elderly people living alone in urban India.

**Page 1 — Introduction:**
> We initiated our research by delving into the concept of social connection and the different forms it takes across generations and life stages. We conducted desk research, expert interviews with gerontologists, and exploratory conversations with seniors and their families to gain a deeper understanding of the topic. Through this process, we discovered that loneliness among elderly people living alone in urban environments is vastly under-represented in public health discussions, despite its measurable physical and mental health consequences.
>
> Our objective is to design a service that addresses the specific needs of urban elderly individuals living alone in terms of meaningful daily connection, and improves their overall wellbeing.

**Page 1 — Global Scenario:** progress in healthcare extending lifespans (acknowledgment) → loneliness is now a recognized public health crisis (pivot) → COVID-19 accelerated isolation (recency) → problem cluster with buckets "Health & Mobility" and "Social Fabric" → 2 stats (e.g., loneliness mortality risk equivalent to smoking 15 cigarettes/day; X% of elderly worldwide report chronic loneliness).

**Page 2 — Indian context:** compares to Japanese / Scandinavian community-care systems → Indian elderly traditionally had joint-family support, now eroding → 7 in 10 urban Indians believe elderly should live with adult children (belief) → 23 million Indian elderly live alone (behavior).

**Page 2 — Impact on Elderly Widows:** narrows to a sub-group (e.g., elderly widows in metros) → causal claim about loss-of-spouse as primary trigger → evidence ladder of stats.

**Page 2 — Interview Insights:** interviewed seniors living alone, adult children, a gerontologist, and a community caregiver → 3 insights covering operational (mobility/errands), relational (cultural shame of asking children), emotional (grief and invisibility) → pull quote from a gerontologist-who-is-also-a-daughter-of-an-elderly-parent → closing nuance paragraphs about how children DO care but feel helpless, and how many elderly find meaning in routine, prayer, or community service.

**Page 3 — Emotional Arc:** 5 stages — Spouse's illness → Loss → Adjusting alone → Slow withdrawal → Crisis point. Descending zigzag with the disclaimer that this is the trajectory for those without strong support networks.

**Page 4:** Wellbeing radar focused on Emotional + Social → 3 opportunities (community programming, technology-enabled check-ins, intergenerational connection initiatives) → HMW: "How might we help elderly individuals living alone in urban India sustain meaningful daily connection?" → 4 impacts rippling from individual longevity → improved family relationships → reduced healthcare system burden → societal model for ageing populations.

This example is intentionally sparse — its purpose is to show that the **structure transfers cleanly across problem statements** as long as the funnel logic, one-two punches, and the closing nuance moves are preserved.

## Scoring Benchmark

Before delivering any brief, score it on the four dimensions below. This is a self-audit step — Claude scores its own draft, identifies any dimension that misses its target range, revises, and only then delivers to the user. The scoring rubric is also presented to the user with the final output so they can see where the brief is strong and where it could improve.

The first dimension is **lower-is-better** (AI texture should be minimal). The other three are **higher-is-better**. Each maps to a specific cluster of failure modes already documented in this skill.

| Dimension | What It Measures | Target |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| **AI-Likeness** | Generic AI prose, filler phrases, melodrama, third-person drift, missing nuance moves, corporate jargon. The brief should sound like a research team talking, not a chatbot summarizing. | 1–3 |
| **Funnel Discipline** | Does each section narrow the noun phrase from the previous one? Global → national → sub-group → individual → journey → synthesis → HMW. No section repeats the previous section's scope. No standalone "Methodology" section. | 8–10 |
| **Evidence Rigor** | Are statistics paired correctly (one-two punch — disparity+severity, belief+behavior, prevalence+cause)? Is every numeric claim sourced to a named, real, retrievable source? Is the evidence ladder visible (each section's stats progressively constrain the population)? Zero fabricated numbers. | 8–10 |
| **Synthesis Sharpness** | Is the HMW question scoped to a *life phase* (not just a role)? Do the 3 opportunities span systemic layers (policy / community / infrastructure) without sliding into solutions? Do the 4 expected impacts ripple outward (individual → relational → family/work → societal)? Is the emotional arc's scope disclaimer present? | 8–10 |

### What each dimension looks like at low vs. high scores

**AI-Likeness (lower is better)**
- *1–3 (target):* Reads like a thoughtful design team. First-person plural throughout. Hedges where appropriate. Bolded phrases are specific concepts, not whole sentences. Both nuance moves (related-stakeholder paragraph + resilience paragraph) are present after Interview Insights. No "delve," "navigate the complexities," "comprehensive," "holistic," "in today's [noun]" filler.
- *7–10 (fail):* Generic, third-person, corporate. Tells the reader the topic is "important" without earning it. Filler openers. Bolded full sentences for emphasis instead of key phrases. Missing the resilience counterweight — reads as a pity-fest. Could have been written by anyone with a chatbot subscription.

**Funnel Discipline (higher is better)**
- *8–10 (target):* Each section's subject is a sharper noun phrase than the last. The reader can name the moment the case study narrows from "working women" to "new mothers" to "Shikha Handa's testimony." Methodology is woven into the Introduction's "we conducted…" sentence and the Interview Insights opener — never its own section.
- *1–4 (fail):* Page 2 covers the same scope as Page 1. There's a labeled "Methodology" or "Research Approach" section. The Indian Perspective section doesn't actually narrow from the Global Scenario — it just restates the global problem in an Indian context. The reader can't tell at any moment which sub-population is being discussed.

**Evidence Rigor (higher is better)**
- *8–10 (target):* Every section has exactly two complementary statistics. Every statistic has a named, real source the reader can find by searching. The evidence ladder is visible on Page 2 — the first stat compares the sub-group to the whole, the second shows how dominant the cause is within the sub-group. No fabricated numbers; if a stat couldn't be sourced, the claim was removed.
- *1–4 (fail):* Stats are unsourced or attributed to vague entities ("a recent study"). Some stats are fabricated. Sections have one stat or three random ones instead of a deliberate pair. The two stats in a section don't actually pair (e.g., two prevalence stats with no severity or cause stat to balance them).

**Synthesis Sharpness (higher is better)**
- *8–10 (target):* The HMW user is a life phase, not a role ("working women who are in early stages of motherhood" — not "working mothers"). The 3 opportunities are directions, each at a different systemic layer. The 4 impacts visibly ripple from individual to societal. The emotional arc has its explicit scope-disclaimer sentence.
- *1–4 (fail):* The HMW is "How might we help women?" (too broad) or "How might we help working mothers in Mumbai aged 28–34 with infants find part-time consulting work?" (too narrow — a solution in disguise). At least one "opportunity" is actually a product idea. All 4 impacts are about the individual. The arc presents one trajectory as if it were universal.

### Scoring output format

After scoring, output a justification block in this format alongside the brief:

```
## Self-Audit Scores

| Dimension | Score | Justification |
|-----------|-------|---------------|
| AI-Likeness | X/10 | [one sentence — name the specific markers present or absent] |
| Funnel Discipline | X/10 | [one sentence — name the noun phrases at each level] |
| Evidence Rigor | X/10 | [one sentence — note source quality + pairing] |
| Synthesis Sharpness | X/10 | [one sentence — note HMW scope, opportunity types, impact ripple] |
```

### Important diagnostic cross-checks

These combinations reveal subtle problems even when no single dimension fails outright:

- **AI-Likeness low AND Evidence Rigor low** → The brief reads cleanly but isn't credible. Pretty prose around weak research. Likely needs more real desk research before another draft attempt.
- **Funnel Discipline high AND Synthesis Sharpness low** → The narrative builds beautifully but lands on a vague problem statement. The reader will feel "all that for *this*?" Tighten the HMW and the impact ripple.
- **AI-Likeness low AND Funnel Discipline low** → The voice is right but the structural argument doesn't work. The reader trusts the team but doesn't know what they're being persuaded toward. Restructure the section sequence before editing prose.
- **Evidence Rigor high AND AI-Likeness high** → The research is solid but the writing is generic. The brief feels like a research report, not a case study. Rewrite for voice without changing the data.

If any dimension scores below target after the first draft, revise that dimension before delivering. Do not deliver a brief with an unresolved dimension miss.

## Operating procedure

When the user invokes this skill:

1. **Verify inputs against the input checklist.** Read `references/input-checklist.md` and confirm the user has provided the mandatory inputs. If anything mandatory is missing, ask for it before drafting — do not invent. If high-value optional inputs are missing, note which ones and explain how their absence will limit the output's quality. Offer the user the choice to either provide more inputs or proceed with a clearly-flagged draft.

2. **Do real research.** Search the web for current statistics relevant to each section. Cite named sources. Do not fabricate numbers. If a needed statistic cannot be sourced after a reasonable search, remove the claim — do not weaken the source citation to fit a number you couldn't verify.

3. **Plan the funnel.** Before drafting, sketch the noun phrase for each level of the funnel and verify it narrows: global topic → national context → sub-group → individual → journey → opportunities → HMW. Show this sketch to yourself (in a planning step) before writing prose.

4. **Draft section by section** following the section playbook above. For each section, output:
   - The prose
   - The required statistic(s) with citation
   - A "Visual elements to design" callout describing what the user needs to design for that section (chart type, icons needed, layout)

5. **Audit against the failure modes list.** Walk through all 12 failure modes documented above and confirm none apply.

6. **Self-score using the Scoring Benchmark.** Score the draft on all four dimensions. If any dimension misses its target range, revise that dimension and re-score before delivering. Do not skip this step. Include the final scoring table in the output to the user.

7. **Deliver as a single document** structured as the 4 pages, with clear page breaks and section headings, formatted in markdown unless the user requests a different output format (e.g., HTML mock-up, designed PDF spec). Include the self-audit scores at the end of the document.

8. **Offer next steps.** Ask whether the user wants:
   - A visual HTML/SVG mockup of any specific section
   - A second iteration on tone, voice, or a specific dimension where they disagree with the score
   - Help drafting Round 2 (solution / ideation phase, which this skill does NOT cover)
